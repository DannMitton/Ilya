# Guide paragraph: the tessituragram (for N.84, Score markup section, where the calibration summary is explained)

Desk, 2026-09-10 23:40, r2 with publication dates at Dann's request. English only; French owed and not written. "Tessituragram" adopted from Titze and Maxfield; "half the singing sits between" coined by the desk. Sources: Titze and Maxfield, Journal of Singing 77(5), 2021, pp. 653-661 (read in full by Sonnet, vocology.utah.edu PDF); Titze, Švec, and Popolo, JSLHR 46(4), 2003 (primary not fetched; formulas from a citing review and E.19).

Every song has a centre of gravity: the pitches it keeps returning to and lingers on, which is not the same as its highest and lowest notes. Ilya draws that for you as a tessituragram, a small chart showing how much singing time the song spends on each pitch, with your own turning points from calibration shaded across it, so you can see at a glance where the song sits in your voice. Under the chart Ilya tells you the band where half of the singing happens, the song's average pitch weighted by how long each note lasts, and the cycle dose, an estimate of how many times your vocal folds will meet over the whole song at the tempo shown. Two of these ideas have names in the research: the tessituragram comes from Ingo Titze and Lynn Maxfield (2021), and cycle dose from Ingo Titze, Jan Švec, and Peter Popolo (2003). None of them tells you whether to sing the song. They give you a picture of the song's demands and a fair way to compare two songs, or the same song in two keys, in your voice.
