## RQ1, "In short", current text
Singing teachers long taught the voice's changes of colour by ear, under inherited names such as "chest" and "head," while the prevailing explanation attributed those changes to the larynx alone. We now know they arise from acoustics (the physics of sound), working together with the larynx and the articulators. When the note you sing meets a resonance of the vowel you shape with your vocal tract, you experience an acoustic event: a turn. Think of a vowel as a shape of the vocal tract. Each vowel shape has its own pitch where this turn occurs, its pitch of turning. Near each pitch of turning you also make laryngeal adjustments, and since you can locate those pitches, you can plan the adjustments. The turn and those adjustments are interdependent, and the familiar break from "chest" to "head" is often provoked right there. Classical singers work to minimize it; many other styles feature it as a colour, from the yodel of country singing to the flips of pop, jazz, and hip hop. Either way, it is not fixed: the vowel you choose and how loudly you sing can move it up or down.

Once you know where your pitches of turning are, you gain technical and interpretive choices. You can let your voice change colour there passively, rather than pushing through into a strident, pressed sound. You can negotiate the turn with deft registration, and let the vowel migrate: with its shape held, the vowel sounds subtly rounder as the pitch rises past the turn, a change Bozeman calls passive vowel migration. Above the pitch of turning you can shape the vowel in more than one way, depending on your voice and the colour you want. MELODY shows you where each pitch of turning falls for the vowels of your song, and which notes sit above or below it.
