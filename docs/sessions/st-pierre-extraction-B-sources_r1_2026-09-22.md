# Brief B: St-Pierre's sources — catalogue and use

Karine St-Pierre, *Pedagogical Guide to the Interpretation of 19th C French Canadian Songs for
Solo Voice and Piano* (DMA diss., University of Toronto, 2016). Page references are printed
pages (PDF page − 11); both are given where useful.

Method note: "Cited where" in Part 1 was located by searching the body text (before the
Bibliography) and the appendices for each source's surname (or, for anonymous/institutional
entries, a distinctive title word). This is a mechanical search, not a read of every page, so it can
miss citations that use an unexpected form of a name (initials only, a translated title, "ibid.",
etc.). Anything not found is marked NOT ESTABLISHED rather than guessed.

### Guides from the census with individual, by-name engagement

**#3 Bernac, *The Interpretation of French Song*** and **#7 Cockburn & Stokes, *The Spanish
Song Companion*** — cited together, p. 1 (Introduction), as examples of the "large number of
voice guides [that] exist that relate to European and American vocal traditions." Supports her
opening claim that European-repertoire guides are plentiful. **Set aside:** implicitly — she uses
them only to establish contrast with "a paucity of guides devoted to Canadian solo vocal
compositions," i.e., she reports they exist and then sets the whole category aside as not covering
her repertoire.

**#37 Schiller, "A Performer's Guide to Works for Soprano Voice by Canadian Women
Composers"** and **#28 Nagy, *A Singer's Overview: Canadian Contemporary Vocal
Literature*** — cited together, p. 1, as "the few guides that exist" for Canadian repertoire, but she
immediately qualifies: they "focus primarily on Canadian art song repertoire in English. No
performance guides exist that are dedicated to French Canadian songs." **This is the dissertation's
stated gap-in-the-literature move** — she reports both guides and explicitly sets them aside as
insufficient (English-language focus) for her purpose.
  - Schiller is cited again substantively at p. 69–70 (PDF 80): "there are guidelines in place for
    rating difficulty levels in instrumental music... Such guides grade music according to
    parameters such as the number of years of study, or the range of the work. Vocal works,
    however, cannot follow a similar pattern..." — she quotes Schiller's own critique of
    instrumental-model difficulty grading, which supports St-Pierre's argument for a
    voice-specific grid. Also listed as one of only three pedagogues (with Hopkin and Carman)

**#5 Boytim, *Solo Vocal Repertoire for Young Singers*** — cited p. 60 and p. 69 (PDF 71/80).
Reported as one of the few guides that "provide subjective explanations as to how they assess

almost impossible criterion to apply... Second, trying to correlate age and level of difficulty is
problematic since all voices are unique." A clean, quotable case of "reports and disputes."

**#19 Hopkin, *Songs for Young Singers*** — cited pp. 70, 72, 82, 83 (PDF 81/83/93/94).

elements that can influence the difficulty level of a piece, such as large interval leaps,
ornamentation, articulation, unprepared modulations, long texts, mature or comical subjects, the
use of an old form of language, and the absence of a prelude." She incorporates elements of
Hopkin's methodology into her own grid (p. 71, PDF 82) rather than adopting it whole, and later
uses Hopkin's own graded repertoire as one of three external sources to validate her grid
