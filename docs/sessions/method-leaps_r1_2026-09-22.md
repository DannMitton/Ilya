## STAGE A. Research

### A.1 Dann's library, searched

**Instrument.** Every PDF in `Voice Pedagogy Library/` and `Voice Pedagogy Research/` (685 files) was converted with `pdftotext -layout` into a scratch folder outside `mnt/` and grepped for: leap, leaping, wide or large interval, disjunct, skip, overshoot, undershoot. 86 files contain "leap". Twenty-five PDFs gave almost no text (scans), so **a scanned book that discusses leaps would be missed by this search.** That is the instrument's known blind spot. The book-length Doscher, Vennard, and McKinney texts are **not in the library as PDFs** (filename search: only Doscher 1992, an article, and Hoch 2017 on Vennard). **Not read.**

### A.2 What each source says, with its instrument

**A.2.1 Christensen 2023, DMA dissertation, University of Toronto.** *The Repertoire of John Beckwith for Solo Voice and Piano: An Interpretive and Pedagogical Guide.* **Read in part this session** (pp. 16-28, his method chapter; the song entries not read), downloaded from `utoronto.scholaris.ca` into scratch and not saved to the tree. **The single most useful source.** He splits leaps into two demands and files them in two different categories:

- **A vocal demand, under Melody, element "Melodic Contour."** p. 17: songs that "use frequent large or awkward leaps or augmented or diminished intervals challenge some singers' ability to sing a unified scale", and his note 22 defines the unified scale as minimizing "noticeable timbral changes throughout their range", "Another term for this is resonance balancing." p. 24: "Wide or frequent intervallic leaps ... can further stretch the singer's ability to sing a unified scale. ... **Phrases which primarily move in stepwise movement in the middle of one's range are less vocally demanding than phrases which leap into the extremities of the vocal range.** The number of occurrences of such intervals adds to the level of difficulty." His footnote 31 on that page cites St-Pierre 2016, p. 75. His scale (p. 26): Moderate-Difficult is "Some use of larger unexpected interval leaps; a few larger interval leaps or sudden movement into high and/or low tessitura"; Difficult is "Consistent use ... numerous larger interval leaps or sudden movement into high and/or low tessitura." **No interval size defines "larger" in this element.**
- **A musicianship demand, under Harmonic Language, element "Melodic Intervals"** (pp. 27-28): "Intervals which move by step ... do not challenge the musicianship of a singer as do larger intervallic leaps such as 6ths or 7ths, or augmented or diminished intervals. The difficulty of correctly singing challenging melodic intervals is compounded when there is no clear sense of tonality." That is a pitch-finding demand, not a voice-fit demand.

**What this gives Ilya.** The vocal half of a leap is where it lands against the singer's own range and whether it asks for a register change; the pitch-finding half is musicianship, which Fable already ruled off Insights for the piano-entrance element (`st-pierre-evaluation-C-fable_r1_2026-09-22.md:112`). The word "sixth" as the start of "large" is vocabulary he shares with St-Pierre and Nix, and it sits in his musicianship element, not his vocal one.

**A.2.2 Christensen 2024**, *Journal of Singing* 80, no. 5, 533-542. **Read in part this session** (p. 539, the passage quoting his dissertation). Same sentence as A.2.1, p. 17. It adds nothing on leaps beyond the dissertation.

**A.2.3 Nix 2002**, *Journal of Singing* 58, no. 3, 217-221. **Read in part this session** (pp. 218, 220 from the text layer; the harvest read it in full). p. 218: "How are the extremes of the piece approached? By leap or by step? Are they part of a scale pattern or rolled over in an arpeggio? How long are the critical notes? ... What vowels are set on the crucial notes?" p. 220, on "Deh vieni": "the highest note is approached by a leap of a major third and is in the middle of an arching phrase"; "several wide leaps, including 6ths, 7ths, and even one minor 9th, offer challenges" (his dashes given here as commas); "In general, the wider intervals are descending, and less experienced singers would need to be careful not to overshoot the lower note of each large leap." **Nix gives the approach to an extreme as context for that note, beside its duration and vowel. That is the attach-to-a-finding shape.** He names sixths and wider as "wide", as vocabulary, and names the descending danger as overshoot.

**A.2.4 St-Pierre 2016**, DMA dissertation, University of Toronto. **Read in part this session** (pp. 75-76, 109, 130). p. 75: "When singing large interval leaps (a minor sixth or larger), vocal fold coordination with ideal breath pressure may become more difficult. Diminished and augmented intervals are often non-harmonic tones and may be considered more challenging to the performer." **No citation on that sentence** (the footnote nearest it, 146, is McKinney on tessitura). Her song prose treats leaps as legato (p. 109, "the interval leaps require some attention in order to maintain a legato phrasing") and names a descending minor sixth as needing "some extra attention from the singer in training" (p. 130, "Ô Canada").

**A.2.5 Bozeman 2010**, "The Role of the First Formant in Training the Male Singing Voice," *Journal of Singing* 66, no. 3 (2010); page range not checked. **Read in part this session** (pp. 295-296). **This is the source that most limits the desk's position.** His exercise progression "from easiest to most challenging" (p. 295) puts leaps FIRST and stepwise ascent LAST: "1) Exercises that leap from a low open vowel [å, as the text layer gives it] to a high close vowel [u]. ... 2) Exercises that leap above a point of turning and back on the same vowel ... 3) Exercises that leap above a point of turning, and then descend with stepwise movement ... 5) Exercises that ascend stepwise ... (This is the most challenging ...)". **For the turn of a vowel (Ilya's turning pitch), a leap across it is the easier approach, not the harder one.** Male voice only, by his title. His complementary strategies (p. 296) include avoiding a crescendo and a breath-pressure increase on ascent. **CORRECTION 2026-09-23, read by the desk from the PDF:** these sit under "Complementary strategies for improving laryngeal registration", training strategies for the young male singer that "have proven helpful in lightening the muscular adjustment of the larynx". Item 1 reads "avoidance of a crescendo upon ascent (a crescendo often is accomplished through a pressure increase)". It is studio advice for exercises, not a rule about a crescendo the composer wrote.

**A.2.6 D. G. Miller 2008**, *Resonance in Singing* (Inside View Press). **Read in part this session** (pp. 102-104, two book pages per PDF page). He uses the leap as a training manoeuvre into the male upper extension: establish F2 tuning on D♭4, then "leap to F4 while F2 stays in place" (p. 102); "the maneuver of leaping an interval while maintaining a tuned formant finds wider application in male than in female voices" (p. 104). **Again a leap as a tool for crossing the passaggio, not as a hazard.**

**A.2.7 R. Miller 1986**, *The Structure of Singing*. **Read in part this session** (grep hits only: pp. 42, 84, 188). p. 42: agility vocalises juxtapose "narrow and wide intervallic leaps", graded "progressively difficult (some patterns are more difficult for one singer than for another)". p. 188: in a stabilized technique the vibrato "will be perfectly regular until a sudden intervallic leap, or some ungrateful passage, presents itself, at which time a tone, or a series of them, will go straight." **The registration chapters were not searched beyond the word "leap"; not read.**

**A.2.8 McKinney**, *The Diagnosis and Correction of Vocal Faults*, p. 194. **Not read; known only through Coates 2016's quotation** (Coates, DMA dissertation, appendix, PDF p. 217): "singing down wide intervals" ... "learn not to maintain so much support after the upper note that he cannot be heard on the lower note; too much support tends to cut off the lower notes". **Descending wide intervals as a breath-energy matter.** Whether St-Pierre's unsourced "ideal breath pressure" sentence comes from McKinney is NOT ESTABLISHED.

**A.2.9 Roth and Verdolini 2014**, "Vocal Health and Singing Pedagogy," in Harrison and O'Bryan, *Teaching Singing in the 21st Century*, ch. 6. **Read in part this session** (p. 85). Uses "singing a leap of a 5th from an /u/ to an /i/ vowel" as the unit of a motor program: interval and vowel change together. **Supports treating a leap and the vowel it lands on as one event.** Gives no difficulty threshold.

**A.2.10 D. G. Miller, Švec and Schutte 2002, "characteristic leap interval"** (through Miller 2000, *Registers in Singing*, ch. 9, and Osborne 2015). **Snippet only.** This "leap" is the pitch jump when a voice flips between chest and falsetto, **not a melodic interval in a score.** It must not be cited for melodic leaps. Named here so no one does.

**A.2.11 Querns Langley 2019**, DMA dissertation. **Snippet only** (p. 72 note 245). Mancini's advice "on how to blend registers when singing over large intervals", and "canto di sbalzo" (singing by leaps) as the tenore serio's demand. Historical support that a leap is a registration matter.

**A.2.12 Ralston 1999**, "The Development of an Instrument to Grade the Difficulty of Vocal Solo Repertoire," *Journal of Research in Music Education* 47, no. 2. **Snippet only (the abstract, from SAGE).** Seven criteria: range, tessitura, rhythm, phrases, melodic line, harmonic foundations, pronunciation. **Leaps presumably sit in "melodic line"; its descriptors were not read.** NOT ESTABLISHED what Ralston says about leaps.

**A.2.13 Arneson 2014**, *Literature for Teaching*. **Not read.** Known only through St-Pierre (pp. 70-71): four sample rubrics, and her example of range ranking.

**A.2.14 Voice science and pitch accuracy, from the web.**

- **Mauch, Frieler and Dixon 2014**, "Intonation in Unaccompanied Singing," *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 136, no. 1, 401-411. **Snippet only** (a fetch summary of the author's PDF, quoted sentences checked against nothing else). 24 singers (14 amateur, 9 professional or semi-professional, 1 none) sang "Happy Birthday". p. 404: "the two largest upward intervals of 8 semitones (minor sixth) and 12 semitones (octave) are significantly flat, i.e., smaller than expected", called "compression" and "well known in the literature (Pfordresher et al., 2010)". **Large upward leaps undershoot.** This is pitch accuracy, which Christensen files under musicianship, and it points the other way from Nix's descending overshoot (A.2.3). Pfordresher et al. 2010: **not read.**
- **Sundberg 1979**, "Maximum Speed of Pitch Changes in Singers and Untrained Subjects," *Journal of Phonetics* 7. **Snippet only (the abstract).** Singers change pitch faster than untrained subjects; untrained subjects drop pitch faster than they raise it; singers do not show that asymmetry. **No threshold that Ilya could use**, and nothing in the abstract on interval size.
- **Herbst 2021**, "Registers: The Snake Pit of Voice Pedagogy, Part 2," *Journal of Singing* 77, no. 3, 345-358, from Dann's library. **Read in part this session** (pp. 346-348). In the zona di passaggio "each larynx has ... the tendency to 'like' to stay in the actually produced register, rather than facilitating a timely register transition", "present in any voice regardless of voice type" (p. 347). **Direction of approach into the zone matters; the article argues this for a stepwise approach, not for leaps.** So it supports direction as a property of the approach, not leaps as such.

### A.3 What the research settles, and what it does not

1. **Every source that gives leaps a vocal cause names a register or unified-scale cause, or breath** (Christensen 2023 pp. 17, 24; St-Pierre p. 75; Mancini through Querns Langley; McKinney through Coates). None gives a measured threshold.
2. **The vocal demand is placed at the landing, against the singer's own extremes** (Christensen 2023 p. 24, "leap into the extremities of the vocal range"; Nix p. 218, "How are the extremes of the piece approached? By leap or by step?"). **This is the desk's first half, and it holds.**
3. **A leap ACROSS a transition is not established as a demand.** The two acoustic pedagogues in the library use the leap as the easier route across the turn or the passaggio (Bozeman 2010 p. 295; D. G. Miller 2008 pp. 102-104). The rubric authors call wide leaps demanding without separating crossing from landing. **The desk's second half, "its own finding where the leap crosses a register transition", fails the test.** The literature does not say the crossing leap is itself the demand, and two sources say the opposite for the vowel's turn.
4. **"Large" starts at the sixth in every source that names a size** (St-Pierre p. 75, minor sixth; Nix p. 220, "6ths, 7ths, and even one minor 9th"; Christensen 2023 p. 27, "6ths or 7ths"). **That is shared vocabulary, never a measured boundary**, and Christensen puts it in his musicianship element.
5. **"Leap" itself starts at the third** in Nix's usage ("approached by a leap of a major third", p. 220), against "by step" (p. 218).

## STAGE B. What Ilya can see

Every line below was opened this session.

1. **The interval is computable.** `VocalLineEvent` carries `type: 'note' | 'rest'` (`packages/score-parser/src/types.ts:450`) and `pitch?: Pitch` (`:462`); `Pitch` holds `step`, `octave` in MIDI convention, and `alter` (`:495-507`), and it is enharmonic-preserving (`:502-506`), so the **spelled interval name** (a diminished fifth against an augmented fourth) is recoverable, not only the semitone count. `pitchToMidi` is `packages/score-parser/src/overlay-engine.ts:66-68`. Insights already filters to pitched notes (`apps/web/src/lib/shane/insights.ts:121-125`).
2. **Ties, grace notes, chords.** A tied continuation is its own event with the same pitch (`types.ts:468`, `TieInfo` at `:572-585`), so it is a unison and never a leap. **Grace notes are dropped by the MusicXML parser** (`packages/score-parser/src/musicxml-parser.ts:702-705`), so a leap approached through an acciaccatura is measured between the main notes. **Chord tones in the vocal line are dropped, first tone kept** (`:707-712`), so an ossia written as a chord tone is invisible. There is no ossia or variant type in `types.ts` (grep, no hit).
3. **Rests and breaths.** A rest is an event (`types.ts:450`). Breath marks and caesuras are articulations (`types.ts:485-490`, `:603-613`). **The crossings row deliberately does NOT reset at a rest:** "Rests do not reset the walk, because the voice still turns across a breath" (`insights.ts:147-148`), a DESK DEFAULT of N.127.
4. **What Insights already flags on a landing note.** Watch kinds: `range`, `crossing`, `cover`, `tracking`, `turnover`, `passaggio`, `timbre`, `sustain` (`apps/web/src/lib/shane/watchlist.ts:97-105`). The passaggio kind fires within ±1 semitone of either typed edge (`watchlist.ts:66-71`, `:396-404`). Findings group by headline kind, **anchored by the heaviest instance** (`insights.ts:250-309`). The singer reads: `rangeAbove` and `rangeBelow` (`apps/web/src/lib/i18n.ts:1496-1497`), `crossing` (`:1498`), `tighten` and `turnover`, both "at the top of your range and sustained here" (`:1499-1500`), `passaggio` (`:1501`), `timbre` (`:1502-1503`), `sustain` (`:1504`). The remaining instances print as "{n} further instances, in the score." (`:1491-1492`).
5. **A leap across a passaggio is already counted.** `countCrossings` counts every move between consecutive sung pitches that puts one below a typed edge and the other at or above it (`insights.ts:142-157`), including leaps. **A separate leap-across-the-passaggio count would count the same event twice.**
6. **How the singer's references are held.** `VoiceProfileSnapshot` has `range?: { lowest, highest }` (`packages/score-parser/src/analysis-types.ts:63`), `tessitura?` (`:69`), `passaggio?: { primo, secondo }` (`:77`). They are **typed by the singer**: `buildVoiceProfileSnapshot` copies `passaggioPrimary` and `passaggioSecondary` only when both are declared (`apps/web/src/lib/shane/analyze-score-adapter.ts:202`, `:211-221`, ruled §B.3). **The type's own comment is stale:** it says the band comes "from voice-type norms and the profile" (`analysis-types.ts:72-73`), which the adapter does not do. So every gate in this method is singer-relative, as the 2026-09-16 ruling requires, but that comment should be corrected so no one builds from it.
7. **Octave reading.** `vocal-octave.ts` shifts a whole line by an octave from the singer's own range (`packages/score-parser/src/vocal-octave.ts:1-24`). A whole-line shift leaves every interval unchanged.

## STAGE C. The method

**In one line: a leap is reported as how a flagged note is reached, on the findings about the singer's extremes and passaggio, and never as its own finding.** The desk's first half survives the literature; its second half does not (A.3, point 3).

### C.1 Which leaps matter, ranked by the strength of their literature

| Property | Sources | Strength | What the method does with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Where it lands, against the singer's own range and passaggi** | Christensen 2023 p. 24; Nix 2002 p. 218; Christensen 2024 p. 539 | **Strongest.** Two pedagogy sources say it outright, both about the singer in front of them | The gate. Only a leap onto a note Insights already flags is reported |
| **The vowel on the landing note** | Nix p. 218; Roth and Verdolini 2014 p. 85; Bozeman 2010 p. 295 | Moderate, and already carried | Nothing new: every finding already names its vowel |
| **Direction** | Nix p. 220 (descending: overshoot); Nix p. 218 (ascending phrases "bring weight up"); Herbst 2021 p. 347; Mauch 2014 p. 404 (snippet; upward large intervals sung flat); McKinney p. 194 (through Coates, not read) | Moderate, and the sources disagree about which way is worse | **Named, never weighed.** The sentence says "upward" or "downward" |
| **What it crosses (a passaggio)** | For: Christensen 2023 pp. 17, 24 (unified scale); Mancini through Querns Langley (snippet). Against, as a hazard: Bozeman 2010 p. 295; D. G. Miller 2008 pp. 102-104 | **Contested** | **Not a finding.** Already counted in the crossings row (`insights.ts:142-157`) |
| **Size** | St-Pierre p. 75 (minor sixth, no citation); Nix p. 220; Christensen 2023 p. 27 | **Vocabulary only.** No source measures it, and Christensen files it under musicianship | **Named by its interval, never thresholded as a verdict** |
| **Landing duration** | Nix p. 218 ("How long are the critical notes?"); Sundberg 1979 (snippet) | Weak for leaps specifically | Nothing new: findings are already ordered by time spent on them |

### C.2 The rules, each with its source or a named DESK DEFAULT

1. **A leap is a move between two consecutive sung notes whose spelled interval is a third or wider.** Source: Nix p. 218 opposes "by leap" to "by step", and on p. 220 calls "a leap of a major third" a leap. Interval named from the spelling (`types.ts:502-506`), so a diminished fifth is never called an augmented fourth.
2. **A rest, breath mark or caesura between the two notes means the second note is not reached by a leap.** **DESK DEFAULT; the literature does not say.** Reason: after a rest the note begins from silence, so Nix's question "approached by leap or by step?" has a third answer, "after a breath", and Ilya should not choose one of the other two for it. This deliberately differs from the crossings row, which counts across rests (`insights.ts:147-148`), because a crossing is about where the voice goes and a leap is about how it gets there.
3. **Ties are one note.** A tied continuation is a unison and never a leap (`types.ts:468`). **Grace notes are invisible** (`musicxml-parser.ts:702-705`); a leap through an acciaccatura is measured main note to main note. Whether that is right is NOT ESTABLISHED.
4. **Attach, do not add.** When a finding's anchor note is reached by a leap, and the finding is `rangeAbove`, `rangeBelow`, `passaggio`, `tighten` or `turnover` (`i18n.ts:1496-1497`, `:1499-1501`), **the finding gets one further sentence naming the interval and its direction.** Sources: Nix p. 218 (the approach to an extreme is part of that note's context, beside its duration and vowel); Christensen 2023 p. 24 (the vocal demand is the leap "into the extremities of the vocal range"). This is Fable's noise point honoured: **one note, one finding, with its approach inside it.**
5. **Do not attach to `crossing`, `timbre` or `sustain`** (`i18n.ts:1498`, `:1502-1504`). Source: Bozeman 2010 p. 295 ranks a leap above a vowel's point of turning as the **easier** approach and stepwise ascent as the hardest. On a vowel-turn finding, "reached by a leap" would read as added difficulty where his pedagogy says the opposite.
6. **No standalone leap finding, anywhere.** A leap onto an unflagged note is not reported. Sources: Christensen 2023 p. 24 places the vocal demand at the extremities; the pitch-finding demand of a wide or augmented interval is musicianship (Christensen 2023 pp. 27-28), which Insights does not report (Fable, `st-pierre-evaluation-C-fable_r1_2026-09-22.md:112`). A leap across a passaggio is already in the crossings count (Stage B, point 5).
7. **Singer-relative throughout.** Every gate is the singer's typed range and passaggi (`analyze-score-adapter.ts:202-221`). No size threshold decides anything: the interval is named and the singer reads it. **"Sixth" appears nowhere as a trigger.** This obeys the 2026-09-16 ruling that a declared voice type plays no part in choosing values.
8. **The anchor only.** The sentence describes the anchor instance, the one the finding already names by measure, pitch and vowel. DESK DEFAULT; the cost is shown in D.2.

### C.3 What the singer reads

The pattern is the current findings' own: second person, the singer's references by what they typed, no verdict word. **No qualitative word is used, so no "as" clause is needed.** "Leap", "upward", "downward" and the interval names are the field's own words (Nix, St-Pierre, Christensen), which meets the 2026-09-22 clarity ruling (`docs/memory/PRODUCT.md:764-785`).

**New string (English; the French is Dann's):**

> `insights.finding.approach`: **"You reach it by {an upward | a downward} leap of {a major sixth}."**

**Attached, as it would print:**

> **m. 60 · F♯2** The note drops below the range you typed. **You reach it by a downward leap of a major third.**

> **m. 27 · E3** This falls near your passaggio; expect the turn to want managing. **You reach it by a downward leap of a perfect fourth.**

Both are the Verdi worked in D.1, against the stand-in singer. The vowel tag each finding already carries is left off here because the hand-working did not run the vowel resolver.

**Standing alone:** never (rule 6).

**Interval names**, from the spelled interval: the standard English names, with compound intervals by their number past the octave ("a minor ninth", "a major tenth"), as Nix (p. 220) and Christensen (2023 p. 25) write them. An octave is "an octave", not "a perfect eighth".

### C.4 Softening

**Recommendation: no softening in the leap sentence, because there is nothing in it to soften.** It states a fact about the score (how the note is reached) and carries no verdict word. Christensen's "specific learning opportunity" (2024 p. 538) is a framing of the whole finding, not of its approach, and a sentence such as "a chance to practise the leap" is advice, which `PRODUCT.md:204-206` rules out ("insight and not intervention"). **If Dann wants Christensen's frame, it belongs once, in the findings heading, not in each sentence.** That is a separate decision from this method.

### C.5 Edge cases

- **Into or out of a melisma.** A melisma is notes without syllables after the first (`types.ts:476-480`). The rule is unchanged: the interval is between sung notes whether or not they carry a syllable. A finding anchored inside a melisma already names its vowel from the resolver. Nix p. 218 treats melisma in the passaggio as its own context; that is the melisma question, not this one.
- **Across a rest.** Not a leap (rule 2). The note still carries every other finding it earns.
- **Onto a turning pitch.** Only `sustain` and the crossing kinds concern the turning pitch, and rule 5 keeps the sentence off them (Bozeman p. 295). If the same note is also near the passaggio and the passaggio is its headline kind, the sentence attaches, because the finding is then about the passaggio.
- **Octave displacement in an editorial variant.** Ilya parses one line; an ossia on its own staff is not parsed, and one written as a chord tone is dropped with a warning, first tone kept (`musicxml-parser.ts:707-712`). **So Ilya measures the leap the main text prints, and says nothing about the variant.** A singer who takes the ossia sees a finding about a leap they will not sing. NOT ESTABLISHED how often that happens; the fix belongs to whatever item gives Ilya variants, not to this method.
- **Across a repeat or a verse break.** Insights reads the performance-order line (`insights.ts:113`), so the last note of one verse and the first of the next are consecutive. Almost always a rest separates them, and rule 2 then applies. Where none does, the leap is real in performance and is reported.

3. **The Verdi range finding is exactly Nix's case.** The lowest note, below this singer's range, is approached by leap, and the sentence says so beside the note, its measure and its length.

### D.2 The likeliest way it misleads a singer

**The anchor hides the leap.** In the Verdi, the passaggio finding prints its heaviest instance, the whole-note E3 in m. 27, reached by a perfect fourth. **The octave leap C♯3 to C♯4 in m. 18, onto a note by this singer's secondo, is the most leap-demanding moment in the aria by every rubric source, and it prints only as one of the "further instances, in the score" (`i18n.ts:1491-1492`).** A singer reading "You reach it by a downward leap of a perfect fourth" could conclude the aria's leaps into the passaggio are modest. They are not.

**The question for Dann, with the cost of each path:**

- **Build neither (do nothing further).** The sentence stays on the anchor; the score view already shows every instance. Cost: nothing. Risk: the reading above.
- **Recommended: add one line to the Insights method note:** "Insights says how a flagged note is reached. It does not list the piece's leaps." Cost: one string and its French. It removes the false inference without a threshold.

The "so" clause is the desk's wording of that contrast.

3. **Everywhere else, nothing.** On the passaggio, crossing, timbre, and sustain findings no source says a leap adds demand, and Bozeman 2010 p. 295 ranks a leap as the easier approach to a vowel's turn (male voices; treble applicability NOT ESTABLISHED). **Rules as restated to Dann (desk proposal, not ruled):**
1. A large leap is two consecutive sung notes, no rest between, a minor sixth or wider.

6. The gate is always the singer's own flag. The interval size is a property of the music, never of a voice type.

## ADDENDUM 4, 2026-09-23 00:30. The rewrite, proposed and not ruled

**The idea.** A leap is worth a sentence only when it lands on a note the singer CAN sing that already asks something of them. Outside the range the note is the problem (Dann, 00:27). In the comfortable middle, a large leap is a matter of finding the pitch, which Christensen files as musicianship (2023 pp. 27-28) and Insights leaves to the singer and teacher. **So the leap matters at the edges of what the singer can do: the top of their range and their passaggi.** Those are exactly the notes Insights already flags with `tighten`, `turnover`, and `passaggio`.

**What the sentence adds there is both perspectives:** the voice gets no climb to prepare the note (Christensen 2023 p. 24), and, at a passaggio, a leap can make the vowel's turn easier than a slow climb into it (Bozeman 2010 p. 295 ranks stepwise ascent as the hardest approach; his article is about male voices, and treble applicability is NOT ESTABLISHED).

**Samples, fictitious, soprano:**

> m. 31 · A5 · [a]. The [a] at the top of your range and sustained here is an exposed spot where the tone can spread or press. You arrive on it by a leap of a minor sixth, so the voice gets no climb to prepare it.

> m. 14 · F♯5 · [e]. This falls near your passaggio; expect the turn to want managing. You arrive by a leap of an octave: there is no climb to prepare the turn, though a leap can let the vowel turn more cleanly than a slow step-by-step approach.

**Gap found by this rewrite:** Insights has no finding for the bottom of the range inside it (Fable, Q5 of `st-pierre-evaluation-C-fable_r1_2026-09-22.md`), so a large downward leap onto the singer's lowest usable notes has nothing to attach to. Nix's overshoot point (p. 220) waits for that finding.

**Dann's words:** *"A leap moves from one pitch-and-vowel combination to another; in addition, sometimes the composer will require subtle dynamics matching (it requires advanced skill to achieve the perception of stable dynamics across radical shifts in pitch and timbre). "Camouflage" is his word, kept after the desk flagged it at 00:37. **DESK DEFAULT on the advice question of 00:37:** his third sentence is the register. It says what the leap allows, never what the singer must do, which sits inside "insight, not intervention" (`PRODUCT.md:204-206`). He can wave this off.

**The method, desk proposal:**
1. **Which leaps.** A minor sixth or wider (ruled 00:27), two consecutive sung notes, no rest between.
2. **What Ilya reads at each end, for this singer:** the pitch; the vowel; where that vowel turns (its measured first resonance, `analyze-score-adapter.ts:103-110`); which side of the singer's passaggi the note sits on; and, once dynamics are harvested, the printed dynamic in force. Reid's three: pitch, vowel, intensity.
3. **When it prints.** Never above or below the typed range (ruled 00:27). Otherwise, when the leap carries an acoustic event between its two ends: it crosses the arrival vowel's turn, it changes the vowel from open to close or the reverse, or it crosses a passaggio. A leap with none of these gets no sentence.
4. **What it says, in his order.** Where the vowel turns. How it is reached. What the leap allows.
5. **Intensity** joins when the dynamics lexicon exists: a leap marked to hold one dynamic across a radical shift of pitch and vowel is the case he names as needing advanced skill.
