## 0. The narrowed question: has anyone placed [ɨ] for a particular speaker, and with what

**Answer: no.** No study found predicts one person's [ɨ] from that person's other vowels and tests the prediction. What exists is measurement of [ɨ] and [i] in the same speakers, and it shows that the relationship between their first resonances is small on average and not stable from person to person.

Reading the table: Padgett and Tabain's nine speakers range from "[ɨ] a shade lower than [i]" to "[ɨ] about 0.9 ERB higher." Near 400 Hz one ERB is about 68 Hz (DESK ARITHMETIC), so the spoken difference runs from about -10 Hz to about +60 Hz, mean about +30 Hz. Mitton's sung-posture difference is +108 Hz, roughly twice the largest spoken one. Two cautions on the comparison: Padgett and Tabain report F1 minus F0, so a change of pitch between the two words is folded in; and they sampled stressed vowels at 75% of duration (preprint p. 9), late in a vowel that glides toward [i].

**What this means for 1.365.** It is one voice's value, in one mode (sung posture, fry), and the only multi-speaker evidence says the size of the [i]-to-[ɨ] step differs between people by more than its own average. Whether Mitton's larger step is a property of sung Russian (a more open, darker [ɨ] by design), of low male voices, or of one singer is NOT ESTABLISHED. No source separates these.

### 0.2 The second resonance

Padgett and Tabain's F2-F1 values (Table A4, stressed) put [ɨ] between [i] and [u] in all nine speakers, at 0.13 to 0.54 of the way from [i] toward [u], mean about 0.27 (DESK ARITHMETIC, in ERB of F2-F1, at the 75% point). Bolla's informant, via the harvest memo: (2,190 - 1,655) / (2,190 - 975) = 0.44 in Hz. Mitton's fraction is 0.67 in Hz. So "between her [i] and her [u]" holds in every speaker found, and the fraction does not hold: it ranges about fourfold. Maryutina et al. (p. 512, citing Derkach et al. 1983, not seen) describe spoken [ɨ] as starting with a low F2 and rising to that of [i], which is part of why a single fraction is unstable in speech. A sustained sung or fry [ɨ] has no such glide; no data on it other than Mitton's was found.

### 0.3 Models

- **Fant (1960)**, *Acoustic Theory of Speech Production*, is built on X-ray studies of one Russian speaker and is cited by Padgett and Tabain (preprint p. 2) as a source of Russian vowel acoustics. NOT READ; whether it gives an area function and computed formants for [ɨ], and what they are, is NOT ESTABLISHED here. It is the first thing to obtain. It would be one more single informant.
- **Story and Titze (2002)**, READ. One man's ten English vowels, reduced to a neutral tract plus two shapes of deformation. Sliding along the first shape from [i] toward [ɑ] raises F1 as it lowers F2 (p. 490, Fig. 3b). That supports the direction of Mitton's finding (retract from [i], and the first resonance rises) for that one tract. It has no [ɨ], and it is not validated against any [ɨ].
- **Sundberg and Nordström (1976)**, READ, perturbed "a set of area functions corresponding to the Russian vowel system" (p. 35) to study larynx height, and found that a raised larynx raises F2 in high front vowels and F1 in open vowels (p. 39). It reports nothing on [ɨ] by name in the text recovered; the figures were not legible in extraction.

### 0.4 Stability across voice types, and fry

No study of [ɨ] across voice types, sexes, or tract sizes was found beyond the nine-speaker spread in 0.1, which is itself the negative result: not stable. No study of [ɨ] in vocal fry was found other than Mitton (2020).

### 0.5 Recommendation on [ɨ]

Ask for it. [ɨ] is the one vowel of the ten where the evidence says a person's value cannot be read off her other vowels with any published warrant. Until she sings it, the defensible statement is a range, not a point: her [ɨ] first resonance lies somewhere from her own [i] value (the spoken evidence) to about 1.365 times it (the one sung measurement), and its second resonance lies between her [i] and her [u]. Whether Ilya shows a range, a flagged point inside it, or nothing, is Dann's ruling.

## 1. The methods

### 1.1 One scale for the whole tract (constant ratio hypothesis)

- **Source.** Nearey (1978), NOT READ, as set out in Barreda and Nearey (2018), READ: a speaker's formants for a vowel equal a shared reference pattern times one speaker-specific factor (Eq. 1, p. 502). The factor is estimated from whatever vowels are in hand; their regression form is built for missing vowels (pp. 506-508).
- **Assumption.** People differ chiefly in tract length, and length moves every resonance by the same proportion (p. 502-503).
- **Error.** The factor is estimated to within about 1% even from one token per vowel (p. 511). What it cannot capture: individual departures from pure scaling, modelled from 98 American speakers as about 3% per formant in position and about 7% in spread (p. 518). Johnson (2018), READ in its PhonLab report form: one-factor methods stay usable with very few vowels; methods with a factor per formant fail badly on few tokens (pp. 75-76).
- **It needs a reference pattern** for the vowels in question. For ten sung Russian vowels in fry there is one: Mitton (2020) Table 5.3.
- **Ilya already does this for the six core vowels**: `stand-ins.ts:18-19` and `tractScale` at `stand-ins.ts:122`.

### 1.2 Position and spread per resonance (Lobanov)

- **Source.** Lobanov (1971), NOT READ, devised on Russian vowels; as described in Barreda and Nearey (2018, pp. 503-504). Her vowel = her mean + her spread × the reference vowel's standard score, separately for each resonance.
- **Assumption.** People differ by a shift and a stretch in each resonance independently.
- **Error.** The spread is estimated poorly: 5% to 20% error even with nine vowels (p. 511). Four numbers from twelve measurements.

### 1.3 Range per resonance (Gerstman) and centroid from corner vowels (Watt and Fabricius)

- **Sources.** Gerstman (1968), NOT READ, via Barreda and Nearey (p. 508-509). Watt and Fabricius, in Fabricius, Watt, and Johnson (2009), READ: a centre built from [i], [a], and a constructed [u′] (p. 420).
- **Assumption.** The corner vowels fix the frame.
- **Error.** Both rest on the two or three most extreme measurements, so one poor corner take moves everything. Fabricius et al. test overlap of vowel spaces in two English accents, never the prediction of a missing vowel. The repository's own audit records that Fox and Jacewicz (2017), NOT READ IN FULL here, argue corner vowels fail to represent a working space (`docs/memory/OPEN.md:4249`).

### 1.4 Ratios and fractions from neighbouring vowels (the current form)

- **Source.** None found. No paper proposes or tests "this vowel is c times that neighbour" or "this vowel sits a fixed fraction of the way between those two" as a way to place a missing vowel. Jones (1953), READ, uses within-speaker formant ratios to describe Russian vowels across three speakers (p. 355) but predicts nothing with them. The method is **unsourced**. Structurally the first-resonance rules are 1.1 with one vowel standing in for the whole tract, and the second-resonance rules are 1.3 over a two-vowel span.
- **Error.** Unknown in the literature. See section 3 for a desk check. One structural weakness is visible in the source table itself: the [ɪ] fraction, 0.39, is fitted on a span of 173 Hz ([i] 1705, [e] 1532; Mitton 2020, p. 82), and fry second resonances repeat only to about ±55 Hz (Swerdlin et al. 2010, Table II, p. 2595). The constant is fitted inside the noise.
- **Does a two-vowel ratio hold across many speakers? No.** The two large American data sets, taken per speaker (DESK ARITHMETIC on the phonTools copies; the papers themselves, Peterson and Barney 1952 and Hillenbrand et al. 1995, NOT READ): the first formant of [ɪ] over that of [i] has a median of 1.47 among Peterson and Barney's 33 men, with nine speakers in ten between 1.18 and 1.88; among Hillenbrand's 45 men the median is 1.26 (1.06 to 1.39), and among his 48 women 1.10 (1.00 to 1.34). The same pair of vowels, the same language, and the ratio moves by a third between studies and by half between speakers inside one study. [ʌ] over [ɑ] and [æ] over [ɑ] behave the same way (the script prints them). A constant such as 1.365, taken from one voice, has no claim to be anyone else's.

### 1.5 Non-uniform scaling between kinds of voice (Fant)

- **Source.** Fant (1966) and (1975), both NOT READ: the KTH server returned errors all session. Second-hand only: Barreda and Stuart (2023, slides 3 and 14), READ, quote Fant (1966) that the female-to-male factor is "of the order of 18% averaged over the whole vowel system," with open vowels' first-formant factor "substantially higher," attributed to a shorter pharynx relative to the mouth. Fant (2004), PART (chapter 2 introduction, p. 15): "tenor/base scale factor relations mirrored those of females/males."
- **Bearing.** If Fant is right, a ratio set from a bass, carried to a soprano, errs most on open vowels' first resonance and front vowels' second. This is the stated reason one bass's constants are the weakest for treble voices.
- **Counter-view.** Barreda and Nearey (2018, p. 503), citing Nordström and Lindblom (1975), Goldstein (1980), and Turner et al. (2009), all NOT READ: overall length does most of the work and speakers compensate for the rest. Barreda and Stuart add that much male-female difference is learned, not anatomical. The dispute is live; its size for singers is NOT ESTABLISHED.

### 1.6 Synthesis

**The distinction that governs this section:** a synthesizer has to make a vowel a listener accepts. It does not have to say where one singer's vowel sits.

- **Formant synthesis (Klatt and descendants).** NOT READ; the citation is from memory and unverified this session. Such systems carry canonical targets per vowel and rescale between voices by a factor. They fall on the listener-acceptance side. That one-factor rescaling preserves vowel identity for listeners is reported by Barreda and Nearey (p. 503, citing Assmann and Nearey 2008, NOT READ).
- **Area-function synthesis.** Story and Titze (2002), READ, is the nearest thing to what Ilya needs in form: a person is a neutral tract; a vowel is a shared deformation laid on it. But the person-specific part came from that person's MRI, not from vowels. The paper states that many tract shapes yield the same two formants (p. 492), so six measured vowels cannot recover a singer's tract uniquely. It also found that changing the neutral tract while holding articulation fixed does not move the formants by a constant proportion (abstract, p. 485; p. 498): a caution against expecting any ratio set to transfer exactly. Story et al. (2018), NOT READ IN FULL (abstract only), rescales that model by sex and age from anatomic measurements; its input is anatomy, not a speaker's vowels. Story (2009) in the library is PART. Maeda's articulatory model was not reached.
- **Finding.** The synthesis literature read here produces canonical or anatomy-driven vowels. None takes a speaker's measured vowels and returns the unmeasured ones with a tested error.

### 1.7 Measure them all (the singing-specific practice)

Titze, Maxfield, and Walker (2016), READ, and Titze (2016), READ: 77 people each produced twelve vowel shapes in fry and measured every one. No vowel was derived. They report that vowel-space area differed between individuals by more than a factor of two (p. 382.e13), that is, singers differ in how far they stretch the tract, which no length factor captures. Miller (2008), PART (ch. 4 pp. 23f; ch. 5; p. 80): fry in singing posture gives F1 and F2 "satisfactory in a practical situation," with "some error always to be expected" because the posture is an imitation.

### 1.8 What is known about fry itself

Swerdlin, Smith, and Wolfe (2010), READ: nine young women, five English vowels, fry and normal voice in one held gesture. Fry raised the first resonance by 45 ± 50 Hz and left the second unchanged at 10 ± 60 Hz (Table V, p. 2596). Repeat fry takes within one gesture differed by -5 ± 25 Hz (first) and 25 ± 55 Hz (second) (Table II, p. 2595). So every fry value, measured or derived, carries roughly a ±25 Hz and ±55 Hz floor, and a ratio applied to a measured vowel multiplies that vowel's error.

### 1.9 Rescaling a complete vowel space against placing a missing vowel

Every normalization method was written to do the first: take a speaker's complete set and re-express it. Whether each can do the second:

| Method | Can it place an absent vowel? | How | Tested for that purpose in the literature read? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Log-mean, one scale (Nearey) | Yes | The model is "speaker = reference × one factor," so reference value × her factor is a prediction. Barreda and Nearey (2018, Eq. 12, p. 507) estimate the factor with vowels missing, and note it can be had from a single vowel of known category (p. 503) | The factor's estimation with missing vowels: yes (pp. 511-514). The predicted value of the missing vowel against a measurement: no |
| Lobanov | In principle | Reverse the standard score: their own "unnormalizing" equation (A1, p. 517) generates a speaker's vowels from a template | Used to simulate speakers, not tested as a prediction |
| Gerstman | In principle | Reverse the range scaling | No |
| Watt and Fabricius | No, as published | It divides present vowels by a centroid; it carries no reference pattern (Fabricius et al. 2009, p. 420) | No |
| ΔF (Johnson) | In principle, as one scale | Reference value × her ΔF | No |

So the extension from the first task to the second exists in the algebra of every method that carries a reference pattern, and the one-scale model is the only one whose authors address missing vowels at all. **None of them reports how close a placed vowel lands to the measured one.** The desk check in section 3 is, as far as this scan found, the only such test, and it is not a publication.

All of them also need the reference pattern to exist. They place a missing vowel relative to a template of that vowel; they do not discover it from her other vowels alone. For [ɨ] the template is one voice (section 0).

### 1.10 Why a synthesized voice stays one voice

Dann's observation: a synthesizer that set each vowel independently would sound like a different speaker on every vowel. From sources read in full, there are two mechanisms, and they are the same idea at two depths.

- **One tract, many articulations (articulatory and area-function synthesis).** Confirmed in Story and Titze (2002): every vowel is the same neutral tract, of one fixed length (17.46 cm, p. 488), with two weighted deformations laid on it (Eq. 1, p. 488). The relationships among the vowels are not imposed; they fall out of the shared tract. When the authors altered the neutral tract and kept the articulation, the result was heard and measured as a different voice quality saying the same word (pp. 494-500). This is the mechanism Dann proposed, and it is real. Its limit for Ilya is in 1.6: the tract came from imaging, and formants alone do not fix a tract (p. 492). Coker's articulatory model at Bell Laboratories ("A model of articulatory dynamics and control," 1976; existence confirmed by a search hit, NOT READ) and Maeda's are the other systems of this kind; neither was read.
- **One factor across a shared pattern (formant synthesis and the perceptual account).** Barreda and Nearey (2018, p. 503): scaling the whole spectral envelope by one factor "generally preserves vowel quality," has been used for decades to change the apparent size of a voice, and they know of no experiment in which the constant-ratio relations among a system's vowels were substantially altered without damage to vowel identity. So the listener-side statement of "one voice" is: all vowels share one scale. The Voder, the Vocoder, Flanagan's *Speech Analysis, Synthesis and Perception*, and Klatt's synthesizer are leads only, NOT READ; what rule each uses to keep a voice coherent is NOT ESTABLISHED here.
- **The perceptual half.** Ladefoged and Broadbent (1957) is NOT READ; Johnson (2018, p. 73) cites it for the claim that the formant range of context vowels influences how a vowel is heard. What was read in full: Nearey (1979, a one-paragraph congress abstract, p. 241) reports that changing the formants of a single context vowel was enough to shift listeners' category boundaries across the whole F1-F2 space, and that a constant ratio hypothesis gave "an excellent fit to natural data." That is direct support for "a vowel is heard against the rest of that speaker's vowels," at abstract length.

**What this gives Ilya.** The coherence of a voice is carried by a common origin: one tract, or its acoustic shadow, one scale. That is a published warrant for deriving a singer's missing vowels from one number fitted to all her sung ones, and against deriving each from a different neighbour by its own constant, which is the "different speaker on every vowel" construction in miniature. It is a warrant for the form of the rule. It is not evidence of accuracy, and it does not rescue [ɨ]: one scale places [ɨ] only as far as the reference [ɨ] is right for her.

## 3. Recommendation

**Build 1.1, the one-scale method, for [ɪ], [ʌ], and [a]; ask for [ɨ] (section 0.5).** Her vowel = Mitton's Table 5.3 value for that vowel × her tract scale, the same number `tractScale` already computes from all her sung core vowels and both resonances. It replaces six constants with none, it has a named source, it uses every take she gave, and it is the method Ilya already uses for the core six, so the ten vowels would rest on one stated principle. Section 1.10 gives the reason the principle is the right shape.

**Cost in accuracy: none established, and no gain established either.** No data exist to test this on sung Russian in fry. As the nearest available test, a desk check on the two large sets of spoken American English treated a handful of vowels as given, took one man at a time as the only informant (as Ilya takes Mitton), and placed [ɪ], [ʌ], and [æ] for every other adult. DESK ARITHMETIC throughout, on the phonTools copies of the data; the script is `research-vowel-synthesis-methods_r1_2026-11-20_check.py` beside this file (md5 c1fda351c58a06b92fe478d0a0faa08b), and it prints the breakdown by sex.

What the table supports, stated at its true strength:

1. On one data set the one-scale method beats the current form by about a third; on the other the two are level. So the change is not shown to cost accuracy, and is not shown to buy it.
2. Every method, including the current one, misses by 5% to 10% in the typical case and by 15% to 25% one time in ten. At 400 Hz that is 20 to 40 Hz typically and 60 to 100 Hz in the bad cases. A derived value is a neighbourhood, never a reading.
3. Reversed Lobanov is equal or best on both sets. It is not recommended: it spends four numbers where one is at hand, its published critics show its spread estimates are unreliable on few vowels (Barreda and Nearey 2018, p. 511; Johnson 2018, pp. 75-76), and it cannot be said in one sentence.
4. On Hillenbrand's set a further run with a reference averaged over many speakers (not available for sung Russian) placed these vowels to 4% to 5%, and the plain average of a speaker's own sex did as well. The largest available gain is a better reference: more singers' complete ten-vowel sets, not a different formula.

Limits: speech, not fry or song; English; no analogue of [ɨ]; one or two tokens per vowel.

**So the case for the one-scale method is not accuracy.** It is that the rule has a source, a mechanism (1.10), and a single statement, where the current form has six constants fitted to one voice and no source; and that the six core vowels already use it.

**What it costs otherwise.** The derived values become less responsive to one neighbouring vowel: a singer with an unusually bright [e] no longer drags her [ɪ] with it. That is the intended trade.

## 4. What Ilya could say to a singer
