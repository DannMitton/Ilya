## NIX 2002. The source St-Pierre took three sentences from

**What it is.** Four categories for choosing repertoire (physical limitations, voice classification, emotional factors, musicianship), then a worked analysis of "Deh vieni, non tardar" against all four. **It is singer-relative from its first page:** "few singers are able to overcome difficulties caused by repertoire that is not suited for their voices" (p. 217). That is Ilya's question, not a grading question.

**What bears on Ilya, with pages:**

1. **The context of the extremes, not only their existence (p. 218).** Nix's questions, in order: are the extremes approached by leap or by step; are they part of a scale or an arpeggio; how long are the critical notes; is the high note isolated or one of several in quick succession; what vowel is set on it; what consonant is paired with that vowel. **Ilya already flags the note. Nix says the note's context is the finding.** This is the strongest case for keeping leaps, as a property of how a flagged note is reached rather than as a separate count.
2. **Low-first-formant vowels on high pitches, treble voices (p. 218).** "Pieces that have high notes on the low first formant vowels [five vowels] tend to work better for male singers than female singers, as such vowels require significant modification in the upper octave of the female voice." **The text layer garbles the five IPA symbols.** St-Pierre's quotation of this sentence gives them as /i/, /ɪ/, /u/, /ʊ/, and /e/; the desk has not checked that against Nix's page image.
3. **The mechanism, stated as a measurement (p. 220).** On "Deh vieni": "these vowels have first formants that are up to one octave below the written pitch. The singer must modify the vowel to match the first formant with the fundamental pitch." **This is Ilya's crossing finding in a pedagogue's words, with the interval named.**
4. **Voiced and nasal consonants on high pitches (pp. 218, 220).** "Voiced consonants can be quite problematic on high pitches"; nasals before vowels on higher pitches need de-emphasis. Ilya holds the IPA of every syllable. **Not computed today.**
5. **Phrase length and rests (p. 218).** A student not yet managing breath across a long phrase needs "repertoire that has phrases of various lengths and frequent rests". And for "Deh vieni" (p. 219-220): "many extended phrases throughout the middle range at a slow tempo", and "the singer must be comfortable with singing for several minutes in this middle region of her voice" (p. 220). **Time in a region, stated as the demand.** This supports Fable's first recommendation (share of singing time by zone) directly.
6. **Full-voiced singing immediately below a major bridge (p. 218)** invites carrying weight upward. A named zone, relative to the singer's own bridge.
7. **Phrase direction (p. 218).** "Ascending phrases invite a singer to bring weight up, while descending phrases help to blend the lighter mechanism downwards." Computable from the line.
8. **Syllabic or melismatic setting in the passaggio and at the extremes (p. 218).** Melisma matters by where it sits, which is r2's melisma adoption stated by its source.
9. **Descending wide leaps: do not overshoot the lower note (p. 220).** A leap's direction changes its demand.

**His bibliography, as leads:** Bunch, Doscher (*Functional Unity*, pp. 195-198), Miller (*English, French, German and Italian Techniques*, pp. 99-136), Titze (*Principles*, pp. 169-178), Vennard (pp. 78-79). All on registers and classification. **Not opened.**

## CHRISTENSEN 2024. The evidence that St-Pierre's grid is the weak part

**Who he is, stated by Dann 2026-09-22:** Christensen was in the doctoral cohort behind Dann's, and is also a student of Lorna MacDonald. **So St-Pierre and Christensen, the two rubric authors nearest this work, both studied with Dann's pedagogy mentor, and both can be asked directly.**

**What it is.** Two small comparative studies of six grading rubrics: Arneson 2014, Christensen 2023, Hopkin 2002, Ralston 1999, Schiller 2001, St. Pierre 2016. Study two had seven Canadian voice teachers grade one Previn song with all six.

**What bears on Ilya, with pages:**

1. **St-Pierre's rubric varied most between teachers.** Rate of variation .29, the highest of the six (Table 4, p. 539); her participants' totals ran from 16 (intermediate) to 39 (advanced) on the same song (Table 3). Ralston's varied least, .11. **This is independent, measured support for Fable's finding that her grid is the unreliable instrument and her prose the reliable one.** It is also the plainest argument for Ilya: a computed measure has no rater, so it has no rater variance.
2. **Grade the elements, not the piece.** Ralston 1999, quoted (p. 536): an overall rating is "helpful in some cases, but a rating of the specific characteristics that contribute to this general rating would be of greater benefit to teachers and students." Christensen agrees (p. 538). **Ilya's rows-not-grade position has published support.**
3. **A flag can be framed as an opportunity (p. 538).** "Perhaps the singer encounters a song in which they are frequently asked to sing in the secondo passaggio, and some sustained singing in a higher tessitura. This identifies a specific learning opportunity." **This is the softening Dann asked for, in a colleague's words**: the same measurement, read as what the piece offers to work on rather than only what it demands.
4. **Criteria-specific scales beat judgement scales (p. 538)**, citing Saunders and Holahan 1997: they "provide a greater level of diagnostic feedback", where a Likert scale "elicits judgements... with the use of nonspecific continua". **Ilya's "as" clause (Fable's pattern) is a criteria-specific statement.**
5. **Range steps named by interval (p. 540).** Easy an octave or less; a major tenth; a perfect twelfth; a perfect fifteenth; two octaves or more. **As thresholds for everyone, refused** (fach ruling, 2026-09-16). **As vocabulary, useful:** a singer understands "a twelfth" better than "19 semitones".
6. **Assessing absent elements lowers a score (p. 540).** Ilya already reports only what fires.

**Where Christensen and Ilya part.** He argues a song's difficulty "should be determined independently of a specific student's ability" (p. 538). Nix and Arneson take the other side. **Ilya is on Nix's side by design**, and Christensen's own study shows why the independent grade is hard to hold steady.

**Caveats, stated plainly.** Seven participants; his rate-of-variation measure is range over possible points, which is crude; and the rubric he finds most promising is his own. He attributes St-Pierre's variation partly to participants misreading her instructions (p. 539). **He also counts her grid as twenty-six elements (p. 536); St-Pierre says twenty-five, and agent A counted twenty-six rows in her Table 3.**

**Leads, not opened:** Ralston 1999, *Journal of Research in Music Education* 47, no. 2 (the least variable rubric, per-element only); Christensen's 2023 University of Toronto DMA (his grid, pp. 16-17, 81, 88, 148, 162-174); Arneson 2014 (four rubrics inspired by Nix 2002, per his note 10); Saunders and Holahan 1997.
