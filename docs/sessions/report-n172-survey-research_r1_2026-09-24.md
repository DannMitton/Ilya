# Report: survey design research for N.172's intake

Researched and written by the desk 2026-09-24, on the brief `docs/sessions/brief-sonnet-n172-survey-research_r1_2026-09-24.md`. Read first: `docs/sessions/draft-n172-intake-survey_r1_2026-09-24.md` and `docs/sessions/draft-curation-rules_r1_2026-09-24.md`.

A note on method: every claim below says whether the desk read the source directly (fetched the actual document or its full abstract) or only saw it cited by another source (in which case it is marked "seen cited, not read" and should be treated as provisional). No citation, page number, or statistic below was invented. Where the literature is silent or thin, the report says so in the NOT ESTABLISHED section rather than filling the gap with a plausible-sounding answer.

## Summary of recommendations, ranked

1. **Keep five labelled points, but know the trade-off.** Krosnick and Presser's own review leans toward seven points as "probably optimal in many instances" (read, p. 274), with reliability rising steadily from two to about seven points and levelling off after that (read, pp. 271 to 272). Five is a defensible middle position, not their top pick. The desk's stated reasons for five (a singer's patience, and the match to Dreyfus's five stages) are legitimate considerations outside Krosnick and Presser's review, which is about general-population attitude surveys, not short self-paced app intakes. Keep five, but log this as a conscious trade-off, not as "the literature says five."
2. **Pretest the anchors before ratifying them, in both languages.** Neither Krosnick and Presser's "equal units" principle for point labels (read, p. 274, citing Klockars and Yamagishi 1988, not read) nor the ITC's cognitive-interviewing guidance (read, p. 14) has been checked against these specific anchors. Run a small cognitive-interview pass with singers (the draft already flags this as open for N.170) and, separately, a bilingual reconciliation and cognitive-interview pass for the French wording before it is written, per the ITC guidelines (read).
3. **Keep the switches on by default, editable, and quietly counted.** This matches the ethical criteria for a defensible default set out in the defaults literature (read, Smith, Goldstein, and Johnson, summarizing Johnson and Goldstein 2003, not independently read): reflects what most users would likely choose, low effort to reverse, and transparent, since it is shown as a visible switch panel rather than a buried setting.
4. **Reconsider the silent "assumes point 3" default on an unanswered question.** This is different in kind from the "no don't-know option" recommendation the draft cites (read, pp. 281 to 283): Krosnick and Presser studied respondents who were forced to pick a real point on the scale, not a system that imputes a value nobody chose. Imputing "point 3, moderate security" for a true novice or a true expert who simply skipped the question could quietly misroute which comments they see, in exactly the area (how hard is this for this singer) the curation rules use the answer for. No direct evidence bears on this either way, so it is flagged as a design risk to raise with Dann rather than a settled finding.
5. **Treat the five-point competency ladder as a genuinely unipolar scale, not an attitude scale, and do not over-cite Krosnick and Presser for that specific choice.** Their unipolar-versus-bipolar discussion concerns attitude items with a real two-sided valence (like versus dislike); it does not directly address a one-directional mastery ladder like Ilya's. The draft's scale is fine, but the report should not claim Krosnick and Presser as authority for that particular structural choice.
6. Continue avoiding "fault," "diagnose," and deficit framing generally. The one existing singing self-report instrument built around deficit language, the Singing Voice Handicap Index, is explicitly a clinical screening tool (read, abstract) and is a poor model for Ilya's tone; Ilya's anchors already read differently, and that difference is worth keeping deliberate.

## Evidence per question

### 1. Scale design

Primary source read directly: Krosnick, J. A., and Presser, S. (2010). "Question and Questionnaire Design." In *Handbook of Survey Research* (2nd ed.), pp. 263 to 313. Emerald. Fetched and read pp. 264, 268 to 277, and 280 to 283 of the PDF at https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf.

- **Number of points.** Reliability rises from two to about five points and continues to rise, with diminishing gains, up to about seven; the increase "levels off after about 7 points" (read, p. 272). The chapter's overall conclusion: "our review suggests that 7-point scales are probably optimal in many instances," with the caveat that "most of the literature on number of points involves visual administration," so applicability to phone surveys, and by extension to a screen-based app, is not fully established even within the chapter itself (read, p. 274).
- **Labelling every point.** "Reliability is higher when all points are labeled with words than when only some are" (read, p. 275, citing Krosnick and Berent 1993, not read), and respondents report more satisfaction with fully labelled scales (read, p. 275, citing Dickinson and Zellinger 1980, not read). Labelling becomes harder to do well "once the number of scale points increases above seven" (read, p. 270).
- **Behavioural versus evaluative anchors.** The chapter's own guidance is to choose labels that "divide up the continuum into approximately equal units" (read, p. 274, citing Klockars and Yamagishi 1988, not read); it does not discuss behaviourally anchored rating scales by name. That literature is separate: Smith and Kendall (1963) developed behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS) using critical incidents and a "retranslation" check, so that scale points are tied to observable actions rather than a rater's evaluative judgement (seen cited via a secondary summary of the original article, not read directly). The logic Smith and Kendall built BARS on, that behavioural description is less ambiguous and less rater-dependent than an evaluative label, supports the draft's rule 6 in spirit, though the draft cites Smith and Kendall the same way, "not read, named from search results."
- **Unipolar versus bipolar.** The chapter frames this choice around attitude items with a genuine two-sided valence, for example "like" to "dislike" (read, pp. 268 to 269), and does not discuss one-directional competency or mastery scales. Its logic (offer enough points to let people who feel mildly positive, neutral, or mildly negative all find an accurate point) transfers loosely to a unipolar ladder, but the chapter does not make that argument itself. This is the one area of question 1 where the draft's implicit design choice, a unipolar ladder from "rarely" to "freely," is not directly addressed by Krosnick and Presser, and the report should not claim it is.
- **Neutral midpoints.** Offering a midpoint "improved the reliability and validity of ratings" (read, pp. 273 to 274, citing O'Muircheartaigh, Krosnick, and Helic 1999, not read); omitting the midpoint pushed respondents to "randomly select one of the moderate scale points closest to where a midpoint would appear" (read, p. 274). Attraction to midpoints is not clearly linked to lower cognitive skill or lower effort across several studies the chapter reviews (read, p. 274).
- **"Not sure" or "don't know" options.** The chapter's evidence, drawn mainly from attitude and political-knowledge questions, is that explicit don't-know options "do not improve measurement" and can function as a satisficing cue, since offering too few scale points can raise task difficulty while very long scales can also raise it, and a don't-know option "may therefore encourage satisficing" (read, pp. 281 to 283). A specific finding: when a don't-know option was removed from later questions in a survey, 85 to 97 percent of people who would have said don't know instead gave substantive, internally consistent answers (read, p. 282, citing Gilljam and Granberg 1993, not read). The chapter's don't-know research is about attitude and factual-knowledge items, where a respondent may genuinely lack the information to answer; skill self-report is a different case, since a singer always has some direct experience of their own current voice, even if uncertain how to rate it. The draft's rule 5 is well supported by the letter of the evidence, with this caveat about the underlying construct.
- **Construct-specific items, not agree or disagree.** This is the chapter's strongest and most detailed finding. Agree or disagree, true or false, and yes or no formats produce acquiescence: on average "14% more people agreed with an assertion than expressed the same view in the corresponding forced choice question" (read, p. 276), and "the reliability and validity of rating scale questions that array the full attitude dimension explicitly... are higher than those of agree/disagree, true/false, and yes/no questions" (read, p. 277). The chapter recommends avoiding agree or disagree formats altogether. The draft's rule 3 matches this directly, and the draft already avoids the format throughout.
- **One thing per question, simple words.** Listed among the chapter's "conventional wisdom" principles: simple, familiar words, simple syntax, one concept per question, and avoiding double negations (read, p. 264).

### 2. Self-assessment accuracy

- **Dunning-Kruger, original finding.** Kruger and Dunning (1999) found that participants in the bottom quartile on logical reasoning, grammar, and humour tasks scored at roughly the 12th percentile but rated themselves at roughly the 62nd percentile, and the effect was more pronounced when people compared themselves to others than when they judged themselves against an absolute standard (seen cited, via a secondary summary of the Wikipedia article on the effect, not the original 1999 paper).
- **Statistical critiques.** Several researchers argue the classic pattern is largely explained by statistical noise plus the well-established tendency for most people to rate themselves above average on most traits, combined with regression toward the mean when comparing noisy self-ratings to noisy test scores (seen cited: Gignac and Zajenkowski 2020, not read). Nuhfer and colleagues' statistical modelling work is reported to conclude there is "no strong tendency to overly positive self-assessment" once these artifacts are accounted for, and that the label fits only a small minority of people (seen cited, not read). This is a live methodological dispute, and the report treats "Dunning-Kruger" as a contested, not settled, effect. Design implication drawn from this literature, as reported by the secondary source: accuracy improves when people are trained against concrete standards rather than judged relative to others (seen cited, Dunning 2011, not read).
- **Musicians and singers specifically.** Two studies were found and read at the abstract or full-text level.
  - Larrouy-Maestri and colleagues, "Are You Your Own Best Judge? On the Self-Evaluation of Singing," *Journal of Voice* (year given by the fetch as 2023; read at abstract level via the ScienceDirect page, not the full PDF). Eighteen trained sopranos rated the pitch accuracy of their own recordings and their peers' recordings in pairwise comparisons. A model built from the peer ratings served as a reference standard. Result: singers were "surprisingly inaccurate" at judging their own pitch accuracy and mostly overestimated it; the authors' stated conclusion is that "singers are not necessarily their own best judges" (read, abstract level).
  - Morijiri and Welch (2022), "Decisions on the quality of piano performance: Evaluation of self and others," *Frontiers in Psychology* (read via the full-text page on PubMed Central, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714486/, summarized by the fetch tool rather than read line by line). Six professional pianists recorded and rated their own and peers' performances of the same piece using repertory grid technique. Finding: self-ratings showed significant individual differences (some pianists were well calibrated, others were not), external ratings were more consistent across raters, and pianists used the same criteria (tone, phrasing, pedalling, musical expression) when judging themselves and others. This supports asking about concrete, named criteria (which is what the draft's anchors already do) over asking for a single global rating.
- **What reduces bias, synthesized.** Putting these pieces together: construct-specific, criterion-based questions (supported directly by Krosnick and Presser), comparison to a concrete standard rather than to other people (supported by the Dunning-Kruger literature as reported), and behavioural rather than evaluative anchors (supported by BARS logic and by Krosnick and Presser's labelling findings) all point the same direction, and the draft's anchors already follow all three. This is the desk's synthesis across sources, not a single citation.

### 3. Skill-stage models

- **Dreyfus and Dreyfus.** The original model comes from a 1980 report to the US Air Force and was elaborated in *Mind Over Machine* (1986). A direct fetch of the original DTIC report was blocked (403 error); the stage descriptions below are seen cited, via the Wikipedia article on the Dreyfus model, not read from the original. Five stages are commonly cited (Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competence, Proficiency, Expertise), sometimes with a sixth, Mastery, added in later writing. The progression described is from context-free rule-following and detached, analytic performance toward intuitive, holistic, involved performance. The model has been criticized (seen cited: Gobet and Chassy, not read) for lacking direct empirical evidence of discrete stages and for underestimating experts' continued use of analysis; the Wikipedia summary reports the original authors' response that this criticism misreads their claims about "deliberative rationality" in expert performance.
- **Berliner (1988, 2004).** Read directly: Berliner, D. C. (1988), "The Development of Expertise in Pedagogy," fetched in full from https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED298122.pdf. Berliner explicitly adapts the Dreyfus model, naming Hubert and Stuart Dreyfus directly, and applies the same five stages (Novice, Advanced Beginner, Competent, Proficient, Expert) to teachers, describing each in terms of rule use, situational judgement, emotional involvement, and the shift from analytic to intuitive performance. Important finding for this brief: Berliner's 1988 paper does not address self-assessment at all. It is a framework for classifying teachers from the outside (through observation, interview, and performance), not a self-report instrument. Using it as the basis for a self-rating scale, as Dann's own outline does, is an extension beyond what Berliner himself built or validated.
- **Benner (1984).** *From Novice to Expert: Excellence and Power in Clinical Nursing Practice*. The desk located bibliographic records (Wikipedia's page on Patricia Benner, WorldCat, Amazon, and several nursing-theory summary sites) but did not fetch or read the book or a detailed academic summary of its methodology in this session, so no direct claim about its content is made here beyond what is common knowledge in the field: Benner adapted the same Dreyfus stages to nursing and, like Berliner, built the stages from observation and interview of practising nurses rather than as a self-report scale. This should be treated as background knowledge, not a verified citation, until read properly.
- **Voice pedagogy or music education use of stage models.** No independent source was found, beyond Dann's own outline, applying Dreyfus- or Berliner-style stage models to singers' self-assessment. This is addressed in NOT ESTABLISHED.

### 4. Onboarding surveys in personalized or adaptive apps

- Searches for peer-reviewed research on onboarding-survey length, completion rates, and optional-versus-required design specifically within personalized or adaptive learning apps returned only industry blog and UX-vendor content (Userflow, Appcues, involve.me, and similar), none of which is a primary research source suitable for citation here. This question is therefore addressed mainly in NOT ESTABLISHED.
- One relevant and well-supported body of research does bear on part of this question: **defaults**. Read: Smith, N. C., Goldstein, D. G., and Johnson, E. J., "Choice Without Awareness: Ethical and Policy Implications of Defaults," fetched from https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/Smith_Goldstein_Johnson_Choice_Without_Awareness_Defaults_JPPM_2013.pdf. Defaults influence choice partly through implied endorsement, partly through loss aversion (switching away from a default feels like giving something up), and partly through the effort of changing it (read). The paper's criteria for an ethically sound default: it should reflect what most people would choose if deciding actively, remain easy to reverse, and be transparent rather than hidden (read). The classic citation behind this literature, Johnson, E. J., and Goldstein, D., "Do Defaults Save Lives?" *Science* 302(5649), 1338 to 1339 (2003), was seen cited within the paper above but not read directly. Applied to Ilya: "all topics on by default, one visible switch per topic, editable at any time" fits this paper's criteria for a defensible default reasonably well.

### 5. Tone

- General, non-performer-specific summaries of stereotype threat (Claude Steele's work) and social desirability bias were found (for example the Wikipedia and Decision Lab overview pages), but no research specifically about stereotype threat or self-presentation bias in musicians' or performers' self-report of skill was located in this session. This is addressed in NOT ESTABLISHED.
- Indirect, general support for low-stakes wording comes from Krosnick and Presser's satisficing theory: acquiescence and satisficing are "most common when... a question is difficult" (read, p. 276) and when respondents feel unable to formulate a confident answer (read, p. 283). This supports keeping questions easy to answer honestly, but it is evidence about survey difficulty and cognitive burden in general, not about shame or stereotype threat specifically, and should not be over-stated as direct support for tone choices.

### 6. Bilingual scales

Read directly (fetched and summarized from the source PDF, not verified line by line against the full document): International Test Commission, *ITC Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Tests* (2nd ed., 2017), https://www.intestcom.org/files/guideline_test_adaptation_2ed.pdf.

- Recommends forward and backward translation as a baseline, but a stronger method is double translation with reconciliation, where independent forward translations are compared and a third translator or expert panel resolves discrepancies working in the target language, rather than relying only on back-translation quality (read, p. 12).
- Guideline TD-2 (numbered guideline 5) instructs adaptors to aim for "functional rather than... literal equivalence" (read, p. 12): the goal is that a French-speaking singer and an English-speaking singer reading their respective anchor at point 3 experience the same level of described security, not that the words are translated word for word.
- Guideline TD-3 (guideline 6) recommends using bilingual respondents to review whether the two versions carry equivalent meaning (read, p. 14), and both TD-3 and TD-5 support cognitive interviewing as a way to confirm that instructions and items are understood consistently across languages (read, p. 14).
- Guideline TD-4 (guideline 7) specifically flags that unfamiliar rating-scale formats, including five-point scales, "can be confusing to respondents who have not seen the item formats before" (read, p. 14), and recommends pilot testing to check this.
- Applied to Ilya: the draft is explicitly "English only; French owed." The recommendation is to draft the French version through functional equivalence, not literal translation, with a bilingual reconciliation pass and a small cognitive-interview check with francophone singers, matching the pretesting the draft already plans for the English anchors with N.170's singers.

### 7. Existing instruments in voice that ask singers about their own voice

- **Singing Voice Handicap Index (SVHI).** Read at abstract level: Cohen, S. M., Jacobson, B. H., Garrett, C. G., Noordzij, J. P., Stewart, M. G., Attia, A., Ossoff, R. H., and Cleveland, T. F., "Creation and Validation of the Singing Voice Handicap Index," fetched from https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/000348940711600602. Thirty-six items, reduced from an original pool of 81; validated on 112 singers with a diagnosed voice problem and 129 singers without one, spanning classical, country, rock, choral, and gospel genres. Internal consistency was very high (Cronbach's alpha 0.97) and test-retest reliability strong (Spearman correlation 0.92) (read, abstract). The instrument measures self-perceived vocal handicap, that is, how much a voice problem interferes with the singer's life, and its own name uses the word "handicap." It is a clinical screening tool for people who may have a voice disorder, not a general skill or security self-assessment, and its deficit-oriented framing is close to the words Ilya's curation rules rule out ("fault," "diagnose," "problem," "warning," "danger"). It is not a good fit for Ilya's intake, and the draft is right not to use it as a model.
- **Evaluation of the Ability to Sing Easily (EASE).** Read at abstract level, fetched from https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199713000209 (development and preliminary validation paper, *Journal of Voice*, described as addressing a gap in disease-focused instruments for tracking subtler changes in healthy singers' voices). The final instrument has 20 items, originally built as two 10-item subscales that Rasch analysis supported combining into one 20-item scale; the working sample was 284 professional music-theatre singers, with an initial set of descriptors refined by 25 performing musicians (read, abstract). The construct measured is perceived singing voice function, meaning how a singer subjectively experiences vocal capability and sensation, including positive change (for example after a warm-up) and negative change (for example fatigue), intended to track vocal load and screen for developing voice problems over time (read, abstract). EASE is a better tonal fit than SVHI, since it is not framed around handicap, but it still measures a different construct than Ilya needs: vocal function and load over time and across sessions for working professionals, not a singer's own sense of security in specific technical areas (top, bottom, passaggio, held notes, soft high singing) for the purpose of tuning which pedagogical comments they see. Neither SVHI nor EASE is a stage-based competency self-assessment; both sit in the clinical voice-assessment tradition rather than the skill-acquisition or self-efficacy tradition the draft is actually drawing on (Dreyfus and Berliner). This is useful negative evidence: it confirms there is no existing, validated singing self-assessment instrument the draft could simply adopt, and that building a purpose-built scale, as the draft does, is the right call rather than a shortcut around an available standard.

## Critiques of the current draft

1. **Rule 2, five points (DESK CHOICE).** Correctly labelled by the desk as its own choice rather than a direct reading of Krosnick and Presser, whose review leans toward seven points as probably optimal (read, p. 274). The stated reasons for choosing five (patience, and the match to Dreyfus's five stages) are reasonable, but they are reasons outside the cited chapter, and the chapter's own review is based on general-population attitude surveys, not short, self-paced app intakes, so the external validity of "seven is optimal" to Ilya's context is itself uncertain. Recommendation: keep five, but do not describe the choice, even informally, as following Krosnick's number, since it is a deliberate departure from his stated conclusion.
2. **Rule 5, no "not sure" option.** Well supported by the letter of the evidence (read, pp. 281 to 283), with one caveat: Krosnick and Presser's don't-know research concerns attitude and knowledge questions, where a respondent may genuinely lack information; a singer always has some direct experience of their own current voice. The draft's own design already provides a substantive floor at point 1 ("I rarely sing there yet"), which functions as an honest opt-out for someone who has not developed the skill, reducing the need for a separate "not sure." This makes rule 5 stronger in context than the general literature alone would suggest, not weaker.
3. **Rule 6, behavioural anchors over evaluative words.** Supported both by Krosnick and Presser's labelling findings (read, p. 274) and by the logic behind behaviourally anchored rating scales (seen cited, not read). The anchors as written ("I can reach it, but it feels unpredictable"; "It is reliable, and I can colour it as I choose") are concrete and behavioural, in line with this evidence.
4. **Whether points 3 to 5 across a question represent equal steps.** This is untested. Krosnick and Presser's labelling guidance calls for labels that "divide up the continuum into approximately equal units" (read, p. 274). Whether "works when I prepare it" to "reliable in performance most of the time" to "reliable, and I can colour it as I choose" are equally spaced, psychologically, for a singer, has not been checked. Recommendation: this is exactly what the pretesting the draft already flags for N.170 should test directly, alongside whether the anchors "read naturally."
5. **The system-imputed midpoint on skip ("Ilya assumes point 3").** This is different from a respondent choosing a midpoint or a don't-know option, both of which Krosnick and Presser studied directly. Here, nobody chooses point 3; Ilya assigns it. A true novice or a true expert who simply skips the question would be treated as moderately secure, in exactly the dimension (how hard the moment is for this singer) the curation rules use the answer to scale. No source read in this session speaks directly to whether an imputed default of this kind helps or harms curation accuracy; it is flagged as a design question for Dann, not resolved by the evidence gathered here.
6. **Unipolar structure.** The scale, as designed, is unipolar (absence of skill to full mastery), not bipolar (a negative pole to a positive pole). This is a sound design choice for a competency ladder, but it should not be attributed to Krosnick and Presser's bipolar-versus-unipolar discussion, which concerns attitude items with genuine two-sided valence and does not address mastery ladders.
7. **Tone, relative to SVHI.** The draft's wording ("I rarely sing there yet," never "fault" or "diagnose") stands in useful contrast to the one comparable existing instrument that was found, the SVHI, which is built around the word "handicap" and clinical deficit framing (read, abstract). This is a point in the draft's favour, worth naming explicitly as a contrast rather than leaving implicit.
8. **French wording, currently absent.** Given the ITC's guidance to aim for functional rather than literal equivalence, with bilingual review and cognitive interviewing (read, pp. 12 to 14), the French version should not be produced as a direct translation pass late in the process; it needs its own construction and testing step, ideally alongside the English pretesting with N.170's singers rather than after it.

## NOT ESTABLISHED

- Whether five specific response points, as worded, are equally spaced psychologically for singers. Untested; recommend pretesting.
- Whether the Dunning-Kruger pattern, in either its classic or its statistical-artifact form, specifically applies to classical singers self-rating technical security area by area, as opposed to global skill or to pitch accuracy specifically (the two music-performance studies found concerned pitch accuracy in eighteen sopranos and overall performance quality in six pianists, not a five-point behaviourally anchored security scale across five technical areas).
- Whether Dreyfus's, Berliner's, or Benner's stage models, all built as external, observer-based classification frameworks, remain valid or accurate when repurposed as a self-report instrument. None of the three original sources were built or validated for self-assessment; this is an extrapolation in Dann's own outline, not a tested precedent in the sources read here.
- Any peer-reviewed research on the optimal length, completion rate, or required-versus-optional design of onboarding surveys specifically within personalized or adaptive learning apps. Only general UX industry content was found, which is not cited here as evidence.
- Stereotype threat or self-presentation bias specifically in musicians' or singers' self-report of skill, as distinct from stereotype threat in general or social desirability bias in general. Not established from what was found in this session.
- Whether Ilya's system-imputed default of point 3 on a skipped question helps, hinders, or is neutral for curation accuracy, relative to alternatives such as leaving the area genuinely unscored. No direct evidence either way was found.
- Whether use of stage models (Dreyfus, Berliner, or similar) for self-assessment has independent precedent in music education or voice pedagogy research, beyond Dann's own outline. None was found.
- The precise content and methodology of Benner (1984) beyond its bibliographic existence and its common description as a Dreyfus adaptation for nursing; the book itself was not read in this session.
