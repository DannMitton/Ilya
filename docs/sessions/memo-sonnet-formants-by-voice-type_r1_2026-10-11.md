# Memo: published F1 and F2 by voice type and vowel (r1, 2026-10-11)

Returned to the desk by the Sonnet helper. Read-only research. No file outside this memo was edited.

## 1. The answer, in three sentences

By the sources I could read, no published evidence I reached shows F2 scaling with F1 across voice types, and none shows it not scaling at the level of numbers by vowel for singers. The one primary source with tabulated singer resonances by voice type (Henrich, Smith and Wolfe 2011, speech values for sopranos, altos, tenors, baritones) shows no monotone relation between voice type and R1 or R2, and its authors report that voice classification "was not correlated" with R1 and R2 for speech, against Cleveland (1977), whose abstract reports higher formants in tenors and lower in basses. A table of F1 and F2 by voice type and vowel for sopranos, mezzos, tenors, baritones and basses in singing was NOT ESTABLISHED: every other source was paywalled, blocked, or reported no such figures.

## 4. What each source says about scaling

- Henrich, Smith and Wolfe (2011), read in the PDF text, pages as the tool reported them:
  - p. 1027: classification "were not correlated with their values of R1 and R2 for speech."
  - p. 1027: "There were no statistically significant differences between the resonances of tenors and baritones."
  - p. 1027: sopranos and altos "differed significantly at the 5% level" (the quoted phrase is a fragment; which resonances it covers is NOT ESTABLISHED by me).
  - p. 1030: "R2 depends primarily on tongue shape and position."
  - The paper contains no sentence relating R1 or R2 to vocal tract length (per the tool's reading; not a full-text search by me).
  - It cites Cleveland (1977) as having found a correlation between resonance frequencies of speech and classification.
- Cleveland (1977), abstract only (Crossref): "higher formant frequencies in tenor timbre type and lower formant frequencies in bass timbre type." Also: "Formant-frequency percentage differences between basses and tenors were similar to those found between males and females." The abstract proposes that "mouth and pharynx lengths" probably differentiate tenors and basses. The abstract implies percentage shifts of formants, which is a vocal-tract-length argument, but I did not see which formants (F1, F2, or all) or the table. Whether F2 shifts by the same percentage as F1 is NOT ESTABLISHED.
- Sundberg (1995), "The singer's formant revisited", STL-QPSR 36(2-3), 83-96 (https://www.speech.kth.se/qpsr/1995/1995_36_2-3_083-096.pdf), read via the fetch tool's extraction: "F4 in singers depends on the vocal-tract length rather than on the larynx tube" (section 2; the extraction carried no page numbers, article range 83-96). This concerns F4, not F1 or F2, and has no F1/F2 table. Not a source for the question.
- Roers, Mürbe and Sundberg (2009), JASA 125(1), 503-512, https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3026326, abstract only (paraphrased by the tool): total vocal tract length varied systematically with voice classification, pharynx length having the strongest influence, in 132 x-ray images from the Dresden archive. No formant measurements are reported in the abstract. It supports the premise that vocal tract length differs by voice classification; it says nothing on F2.
- To (2010), University of Hong Kong B.Sc. thesis (https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/173725), abstract only: 25 sopranos, 11 mezzos, 17 tenors, 12 baritones; F1 to F3 of four vowels; "Sopranos generally had higher formant frequencies than mezzo-sopranos" (the tool paraphrased; this is not a verbatim quote). No numbers seen.
- Yan, Xue and Mok (2011), IEEE BMEI, https://doi.org/10.1109/BMEI.2011.6098560, abstract only: 27 sopranos, 23 mezzos; sopranos had shorter, smaller vocal tracts and generally higher formants; the authors suggest volume, not only length, may matter. No numbers seen.
- Bloothooft and Plomp (1984 to 1986, JASA, Spectral analysis of sung vowels I to III; DOIs 10.1121/1.390732, 10.1121/1.392001, 10.1121/1.393423), abstracts only: one-third-octave spectra, not formant tables. Part III says interindividual spectral differences among males, "like the average difference between males and females, arise mainly from differences in vocal tract dimensions." No F1 or F2 by voice type.
- Hillenbrand et al. (1995): no scaling statement was read; only the dataset means above.
