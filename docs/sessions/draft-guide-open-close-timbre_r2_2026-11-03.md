## What is fR1?

fR1 is the first resonance of the vocal tract for the vowel being sung: the lowest frequency a given vowel shape amplifies. Each vowel has its own fR1, and the shape of the vowel, not the note, decides where it sits. Bozeman ties a well-placed turn to retaining depth (tube length), vowel shape, and relative vowel closeness, which together maintain the fR1 location while the pitch moves (2013, 23, quoted in Mitton 2020, § 1.7). In my own low male voice, dark-a [ɑ] resonates at about E♭5 and [i] at about D4 (Mitton 2020, § 3.2.5 and Conclusions). fR1 is a frequency; writing it as a pitch on the staff is a convenience (§ 5.3.3).

## What is the pitch of turning?

The pitch of turning is a sung pitch: the note an octave below a vowel's fR1, where the second harmonic of the sung tone meets fR1. The term is Bozeman's name for that transition (Howell 2025, 151). "The sung pitch an octave below my fR1 values is the acoustic pitch of turning between open and close timbre" (Mitton 2020, § 5.3.3). For [ɑ], with its fR1 at E♭5, the pitch of turning is E♭4.

fR1 and the pitch of turning are easily conflated, yet they name two things an octave apart: a property of the vowel, and the place on the staff where that property becomes audible. Measured against the vowel's speech resonance, Bozeman places the turn "slightly less than an octave below," and reads a turn at its predicted place as evidence of a stable tube length (2013, 45).

## Why an octave below?

Every sung pitch (fo) carries a harmonic series: fo, 2fo, 3fo, and so on. The second harmonic, 2fo, sounds an octave above the sung pitch, so at the pitch of turning 2fo reaches fR1. Bozeman calls the second harmonic's rise through the first formant "the primary acoustic registration event of male voices" (2013, 23). Every harmonic that crosses fR1 has an audible effect, a closing as it rises through and an opening as it drops below, and the location of each crossing can be predicted (2013, 45).

## What are open and close timbre?

Howell gives the two states in a line of notation: open timbre is 2fo < fR1, and close timbre is fo < fR1 < 2fo (2025, 151, after Bozeman). In open timbre, two or more harmonics lie at or below fR1, and "the more harmonics below the first formant, the more open the timbre" (Bozeman 2013, 21). "The further below fR1 that the sung pitch sounds, the more open and complex the sung tone is perceived to be" (Mitton 2020, § 1.7). Once 2fo rises above fR1, the timbre is heard to close or turn over: close [kloʊs] timbre, *voce chiusa*, described as domed, tipped, smoother, higher in sensation in the head, and somewhat covered (Bozeman 2013, 23). For [ɑ] in my voice, then, pitches below E♭4 sound in open timbre and pitches from E♭4 toward E♭5 in close timbre.

Close timbre is an acoustic matter and is not to be confused with a closed vowel, which is a matter of diction: the tongue's height in [e] as against [ɛ] (Mitton 2020, § 1.7). The boundary between open and close also moves with the vowel, so no single pitch divides them for every vowel (§ 3.2.5). Low male voices spend most of their phonation in open timbre, which coincides with the prevalence of [ɑ] in sung Russian and its high fR1 (Conclusions).

## What happens at fR1 itself, and above it?

When the sung pitch itself reaches fR1, Bozeman describes a strong F1/H1 coupling, called whooping or hooting: a full, deep timbre dominated by the fundamental, smoother and less brassy than open timbre (2013, 23 to 24). Above that point the singer may let fR1 track the rising pitch, which I call whoop coupling (Mitton 2020, § 3.2.5). In the low male voice this is rare: in the repertoire I studied, only [i] at or above about D4 reaches it (Conclusions). Its counterpart lower down is yell timbre, where fR1 is raised to keep tracking 2fo above the vowel's usual fR1; Western classical singing avoids it (Bozeman 2013, 21).

Smaller turnings also occur below the pitch of turning. The third harmonic crosses the fR1 of the open vowels [ɛ], [ɔ], and [ɑ] just below the primo passaggio, a "mini-turning" that singers often feel as narrower and taller, and the fourth harmonic rehearses the primary turn an octave lower (Bozeman 2013, 46). In my own voice I locate these at an octave and a fifth, two octaves, and two octaves and a major third below fR1 (Mitton 2020, § 3.2.5).

## How does MELODY show this?

MELODY follows the conventions of Appendices B and C (Mitton 2020). Each sung note carries a stemless lavender notehead: the pitch of turning for that note's vowel, taken from your voice profile. fR1 itself is not drawn. The stem compares the note with its own lavender head. A note at or above its lavender head sounds in close timbre and takes a stem up; a note below its lavender head, by any distance, sounds in open timbre and takes a stem down. A semitone below the pitch of turning is still open. A place where the sung pitch meets fR1 is one of the moments MELODY boxes in red. These marks are a model of your voice, and every model leaves something out (Howell 2025, 15 to 16).
