## Question 1. Benefits and purposes of dose; what a dose means; what makes it meaningful

### Titze, Švec, and Popolo (2003), J Speech Lang Hear Res 46(4). Speech data from six readers.

Purpose and framing:

- “Excessive vibration of vocal fold tissues due to loud or prolonged vocalization has been assumed to contribute to development of voice disorders and presents a significant health concern.” (PDF p. 2)
- “Excessive voicing can be thought of as an exposure problem. Although the vibration exposure is self-induced, it in many ways resembles exposure to sun rays or chemicals. The surface tissues of the vocal folds are traumatized by rapid accelerations and decelerations, or by contact forces between the two vocal folds. Generally, exposure is quantified by dose.” (PDF p. 3; manuscript p. 2)
- “The simplest vocal dose is the time dose, often called the voicing time, which accumulates the total time the vocal folds vibrate during speech. Rantala and Vilkman (1999) offered another dose, the vocal loading index (VLI), which measures the total number of oscillatory cycles performed in the vocal folds during vocalization.” (PDF p. 3)
- “Rantala and Vilkman reported that the VLI correlated moderately well with subjective vocal complaints in female teachers.” (PDF p. 3)

Why more than time, and what cycles do and do not capture:

- “The results showed that large F0 and SPL variations in speech affected the dose measures, suggesting that accumulation of phonation time alone is insufficient.” (PDF p. 2)
- “Note that the distance dose and the dissipation dose account for both the F0 and intensity variations in phonation, whereas the radiation dose is sensitive only to the voice intensity, the VLI is sensitive only to F0, and the time dose is sensitive to neither F0 nor SPL.” (PDF p. 9; manuscript p. 8)
- “Rantala and Vilkman (1999) hypothesized that the total VLI dose could be seen as an indicator of total number of vocal fold collisions causing damage to vocal fold tissues.” (PDF p. 13)
- “Rapid acceleration and deceleration (a kind of ‘whiplashing’ of the tissue), as well as the repetitive collisions of the vocal folds, would appear to pose the greater threat of injury.” (PDF p. 15)

Points of comparison (norms and limits), with the authors' own caveats:

- “Preliminary normative values of the exposure per second of normal speech are derived and the variability of the doses with gender and different speech inflections is studied.” (PDF p. 3)
- “The vibration exposure of the vocal folds in normal speech was related to the industrial limits for hand-transmitted vibration, in which the safe distance dose was derived to be about 500 m. This limit was found rather low for vocalization; it was related to a comparable time dose of about 17 min of continuous vocalization, or about 35 min of continuous reading with normal breathing and unvoiced segments.” (PDF p. 2)
- “This safety limit of about 520 m accumulated distance can serve as a good starting point when quantifying the exposure of vocal folds to vibration. As the differences in hand and vocal fold tissue morphology (and their response to vibrational stress) become clearer, the safety limits will likely undergo refinement.” (PDF p. 7)
- “It was found that vocal folds travel about 0.5 m per second of continuous phonation, which suggests that the safety distance of 520 m can be reached after 17 min of continuous vocalization. Because this seems unusually stringent according to personal experience, the silent pauses in vocalization are hypothesized to be important for raising the safety limit and prolonging the safe performance time.” (PDF p. 14)
- On recovery: “Unfortunately, the recovery effect of the voicing pauses, as well as the quantitative effect of the different structure on the safety limit, are totally unknown at this point.” (PDF p. 14)
- On voice type: “As expected, the VLI in Figure 8b, which is directly related to F0, was almost twice as high for women (c. 7,000–12,000 cycles) than for men (c. 4,000–8,000 cycles).” (PDF p. 12; manuscript p. 11)

READER'S NOTE: all data in this paper are from reading aloud (about 63 to 105 seconds each), not singing. The 520 m figure is borrowed from hand-tool standards and the authors call it unusually stringent. Cycle dose is higher for higher voices on the same duration, so a cycle dose is only comparable within a voice type, or after conversion to distance dose.

### Titze (2008), J Singing 65(1). Short column.

- “Recent investigations on vocal dosimetry conducted on teachers in public schools are based on four formal definitions of vocal doses: time dose, cycle dose, distance dose, and energy dose. [...] Two of the doses calculated, the time dose and the cycle dose, appear to be useful for quantifying a singer’s tessitura.” (p. 59)
- “where tp is the duration of time accumulated on each pitch over the entire song and Fp is the fundamental frequency of a given pitch in the song. The time dose can be measured in seconds of actual performance or in equivalent whole notes presented in the score. The cycle dose is measured in cycles of vibration of the vocal folds.” (p. 59)
- “It is this cycle dose that gives the greatest clue to the tessitura for a singer.” (p. 60)
- “unless the accumulated duration and cycle doses are quantified on various pitches within the song, singer-song adaptation still may not be complete.” (p. 60)
- Limit of the score-based method: “If there are unwritten notes and fermatas, the distributions can be obtained only with a dosimeter during performance.” (p. 59)

READER'S NOTE (arithmetic, not an authorial claim): the cycle counts printed on p. 60 (5584 on F4, 4471 on E-flat 4, 2825 on B-flat 3) equal the pitch frequency multiplied by the number of whole notes (for example, 16 whole notes times about 349 Hz is about 5587), not by seconds. The same page says F4 sounds for 40 seconds at 96 beats per minute, which would give about 13,970 cycles. So the printed figures read as a relative index in "whole-note units". Nix (2014, p. 564) and Gaskill (2013, p. 12) compute cycle dose as Hz times seconds.

### Apfelbach (2020), J Voice 36(5)

- Definition used: “Multiplying F0 by note duration for each voiced pitch, then summing across pitches yields an estimate of the total number of vocal fold cycles in a piece. This vocal fold cycle total can then be divided by the piece’s total duration in order to calculate the mean number of vocal fold cycles per second. Both vocal fold metrics act as indices of vibratory dose.” (p. 601)
- What each metric answers: “Percent time spent voicing is sensitive to voicing duration and rest duration, for example, but not to F0; total vocal fold cycle values are sensitive to voicing duration and F0, but not to rest duration; mean vocal fold cycle values are sensitive to all of the above factors.” (p. 603)
- Point of comparison: other pieces in the same work. “Furthermore, ‘Gefrorne Tränen’ boasts low values for percent time spent voicing, estimated vocal fold cycles, and mean vocal fold cycles per second relative to other pieces within the cycle, enhancing confidence that the overall vocal fold vibratory dose and risk of phonotrauma is small.” (p. 603)
- Pacing meaning: “‘Wasserflut’ achieves its high phonatory dose at a marathon pace, rather than at a sprint pace, as is the case for ‘Der stürmische Morgen.’” (p. 605)
- Purpose: “Tessa’s visualizations of the tessitura and vocal demand macrostructures of operas, shows, recitals, concerts, etc. may help singers gauge optimal levels of vocal exertion.” (p. 606)
- Comparison with the singer: “Comparing a singer’s current vocal abilities (as described by the VRP) with a piece’s vocal demands (as described by the tessiturogram and vocal fold vibratory dose data) may therefore allow students, teachers, and clinicians to determine the optimal moment to progress into newer, more challenging repertoire.” (p. 606)
- Caveat: “Although a gestalt understanding of vocal demand, the factors that comprise it, and the idiosyncratic responses of different voice users to given levels of vocal demand is still emerging, more and more tools are available for quantifying individual aspects of vocal demand” (p. 605). Also: “Tessa cannot parse dynamic markings” (p. 606).

READER'S NOTE: Apfelbach gives no external norm. The comparison class is the piece's neighbours in the same cycle.

### Sandage and Hoch (2019), J Singing 75(4). One baritone.

- Definition (note the different use of "vocal dose"): “Vocal dose, a term interchangeable with distance dose, is defined as the approximate distance the vocal folds travel over a period of time, the distance (or dose) of which is calculated from frequency and intensity measures that are taken at regular intervals (e.g., every second).” (p. 396)
- Purpose: “Understanding the workload required for a given vocal effort, such as a recital, can guide the type of voice building that will be required to offset vocal fatigue.” (p. 396)
- Norms offered: “Recent research efforts have quantified vocal dose for elementary school classroom teachers and elementary school music teachers, determining that the vocal folds traveled about 3500 meters and 7000 meters, respectively.” (p. 396)
- The recital's values (Table 3, p. 397): rehearsal 2954.56 meters (42.21 meters/min); recital 3098.18 meters (42.44 meters/min).
- Comparison drawn: “The recital performance required a similar distance dose as elementary school classroom teachers; however, more than 80% of the time was spent voicing for the recital compared to less than 20% of time spent voicing over the entire work day for the classroom teachers.” (pp. 398 to 399)
- Use of dose as a control: “APM data were collected at two different times to verify that the vocal dose was similar across trials.” (p. 397)
- Forward purpose: “Future research efforts to expand our understanding of target vocal dose and vocal power requirements for specific repertoire will support development of fatigue-resistance training considerations.” (p. 399)

READER'S NOTE: the recital's 3098 m is about six times the 520 m "safe" figure in Titze et al. (2003); Sandage and Hoch do not mention that limit.

### Gaskill, Cowgill, and Many (2013), J Singing 70(1). Six undergraduates, five days.

- Purpose of dosimetry: “It is important for voice teachers to be aware of how much a young singer is using his/her voice.” (p. 13)
- What cycle dose is: “The cycle dose is simply an estimate of the total number of cycles of vocal fold tissue oscillation, calculated from both the elapsed phonation time data and the average fundamental frequency for the time period of interest (for example, 5 minutes or 300 seconds of phonation at an average fundamental frequency of 200 Hz or cycles per second would result in a cycle dose of 60,000 cycles).” (p. 12)
- Voice-type difference: “Obviously, the cycle dose is sensitive to average F0 or pitch of the voice, so that one would assume that, in general, females (speakers or singers) would incur higher cycle doses for the same elapsed phonation time than would males.” (p. 12)
- What distance dose adds: “The distance dose, then, is particularly interesting since it is sensitive not only to pitch but also to loudness, such that loud singing (associated with greater amplitude of vocal fold tissue vibration) would result in higher distance doses, and high-pitched, loud singing would result in still higher distance doses.” (p. 12)
- Norms: “Since vocal dosimetry is in its infancy, no ‘norms’ for vocal dose values currently exist in terms of either accumulated or percent phonation time for any population, singers or speakers.” (pp. 12 to 13)
- Comparability: “Data for cycle and distance doses haven’t been reported by all researchers or in the same way, so it is difficult to begin comparing these values across studies or make any generalizations to any study population at large.” (p. 13)
- Safety limit, qualified: “it is clear both from theoretical calculations and vocal dosimetry data that the vocal folds routinely exceed the reported upper safety limit for tissue vibration.” (p. 12)
- Hoped-for use: “larger data sets can be established that may provide benchmarks for healthy vocal dose levels in singers across genres and professions.” (p. 18)
- Their data: mean daily distance dose 5403 m, mean weekly 24,975 m, mean cycle dose per hour 119 kCycles (Tables 3 and 4, p. 16).
- What dose cannot say alone: “Yet experience tells us that there are plenty of singers and teachers who seem to vocally fatigue or even sustain vocal injury after incurring comparatively lower vocal doses.” (p. 12)
- A dose is not just singing: “These two examples demonstrate how the most vocally demanding times for students may often be outside of required singing activities, and that they may be very poor judges of their own perceived vocal doses.” (p. 17)

### Nix (2014), J Singing 70(5). One soprano, one Mozart song.

- Lineage: “Titze improved upon Thurmer’s work by adding to the process the duration of each sung pitch (based upon the written rhythmic values and a metronome setting) and the frequency in Hertz of each pitch. By doing so, time and cycle doses for each note and for each phrase, aria, song, or even stage role could be precisely calculated. Titze then compared these doses against a singer’s VRP to determine whether or not the dose-calculated piece might be suitable for the singer.” (p. 563)
- Accuracy of score-estimated doses (Table 3, p. 569): estimated time dose 98.5 s against 107.6 s (+9.2%) and 105.4 s (+7%); estimated cycle dose 46,801.9 against 50,296.4 (+7.5%) and 49,100.3 (+4.9%). “While the time and cycle dose estimates were relatively accurate, the recovery estimates were substantially less than the actual values.” (p. 569)
- What the cycle dose adds over time: “The higher F0 increases the ‘cost’ of the note in terms of vibration exposure to the singer versus the time the tissue is in vibration.” (p. 566). Example in the text: the time-dose ratio of B[flat]4 (25 s) to E[flat]5 (13 s) is 1.92 to 1, while the cycle-dose ratio is 1.44 to 1 (11654 to 8089 cycles). (p. 566)
- Limit on distance dose in singers: “Any distance dose estimates or measurements on singers derived from SPL levels must take into consideration the possibility for substantial errors.” (p. 571)
- Prior dosimetry of singers: “Results indicated that increased vocal loading episodes coincided with more harsh subjective ratings of voice quality on the day of the load and between 24–72 hours afterward, and that vocal rest periods prior to a loading episode resulted in improved subjective ratings after the load.” (p. 562, describing Carroll et al., 2006)
- Practicality: “The methods used in this pilot study, particularly performing the tessituragram estimates, were labor intensive, and would require additional refinement before they could be practical for voice instructors and performers to use. Perhaps an estimation method could be developed for automatically extracting pitch and rest occurrences and durations using information from digital files of musical scores (e.g., Finale, Sibelius, MIDI); if so, the tessituragram procedure could be accomplished relatively simply.” (p. 570)

### Willis, Tagirisa, and Sandage (2025), J Voice, in press. Four soprano scores.

This paper does not compute cycle or distance dose. It computes related score measures.

- Purpose: “identifying the bioenergetic profile of task demands is beneficial in building an effective training plan that allows for injury prevention and fatigue management.” (p. 1)
- Measures used: “Each score was analyzed for phonation and rest duration (seconds), number of fundamental frequency changes (hertz/second), and speed of dynamic changes when written (decibels sound pressure level/second).” (p. 1)
- Why a voice:rest ratio: “To mirror exercise science metrics, an exercise:rest ratio was calculated as the voice:rest ratio for each song to aid in identification of bioenergetic systems being utilized.” (p. 3)
- Limit: “The operational definitions used for this analysis, while helpful in providing a starting place and interpretation, also offer limitations in that these were not defined based on vocal physiological evidence.” (pp. 8 to 9)
- Calls for dosimetry: “analysis of dosimetry data collected during performances of analyzed scores would provide crucial information regarding the validity of score analysis and functional data of real-world demands compared with theoretical demands.” (p. 9)

### Papers with nothing direct on Q1

- Titze (1983, Nov/Dec) Part II, pp. 29 to 30: no dose measure, but relevant to why pitch matters. “if the vocal configuration (length, thickness, depth, vibrational amplitude, etc.) were to remain constant over the entire pitch range, a one-octave pitch rise would produce a four-fold increase in energy dissipation, and a two-octave pitch rise would produce a sixteen-fold increase.” (p. 29). ## Question 2. Time per vowel, time per pitch, the duration-weighted distribution of a song

### Time per vowel: nothing found in any of the thirteen papers

No paper measures or recommends measuring phonation time per vowel. The nearest statements:

- Nix (2014): “(3) The vowels on the highest/lowest pitches. Given formant locations and tuning strategies employed by successful singers, preferred vowels for extreme notes are very much a gender specific issue. A brief written comment listing the vowels on highest and lowest notes provided as a supplement to a tessituragram would enhance the tessituragram’s value to teachers and singers more than any numeric value or graph.” (p. 570)
- Nix (2014), on why a vowel-blind laboratory profile can mislead: “the performance was on a variety of consonants and vowels, while the VRP was on the /a/ vowel only.” (p. 568)
- Elliott (2004), a singer's reply: “A high tessitura feels different depending on the language, vowels in particular, the orchestration, and tempi. It also depends on the kind of line being sung.” (p. 245). The same remark is repeated as a “tessitura tint” (p. 248).
- Apfelbach (2020) says the program “extracts pitch, duration, and lyric information” (p. 599) but reports no vowel-level output.

READER'S NOTE: Nix and Elliott treat vowel as a modifier of how a pitch feels or is produced, and Nix proposes a written note rather than a number. Neither claims a per-vowel time measure has a benefit.

### Titze (2008)

- Definition: “Thus, the tessitura of a song tends to be associated with the distribution of pitches, measured in frequency of occurrence and duration of each occurrence. Similarly, the tessitura of a singer relates to the comfort level with a given distribution of pitches in a song.” (p. 59)
- Count versus duration: “Although C4 has the greatest number of occurrences, F4 (the dominant) has the greatest accumulated duration (time dose), the equivalent of 16 full notes, or 4 full measures.” (p. 60)
- Claimed benefit: “We conclude that the tessitura of a singer may not match the tessitura of the song, even though the range is appropriately matched. Technology may provide some help in structuring tessituragrams to find the best matches.” (p. 60)
- Pairing with the singer's voice range profile: “One could easily argue that the ideal tessitura for the singer would be centered around the pitch where the greatest dynamic range is available. This is where the singer can most easily crescendo and decrescendo, allowing for maximum interpretive skills.” (p. 60)
- The example: “An approximate 30 dB of dynamic range is available, allowing for dynamic markings of pp, p, mp, mf, f, and ff to be differentiated by about 6 dB. In the D4–G4 range, only a 10–15 dB dynamic range is available to this singer, limiting the differentiation of dynamic marking to only about 3 dB.” (p. 60)

### Apfelbach (2020)

- Definition of the object: “tessiturograms are matrices or tables that contain the total duration of voicing (usually given as a multiple of the smallest note duration present in the piece, eg, ‘64 16th-notes on A3, 30 16th-notes on B3,’ etc.) at each pitch within a piece of music.” (p. 600)
- Quoting Grove: “The tessitura of a piece is not decided by the extremes of its range, but rather by which part of the range is most used.” (p. 599)
- Claimed benefit: “Once completed, tessiturograms reveal much about a piece’s textural character: does the tessitura sit within a comfortable modal register with only a few outlying notes at the extreme highs or lows of the pitch range, or is it sustained in a register too high or too low for a singer to approach comfortably over hours of performance? If transposing the piece to a different key is artistically feasible, would such a transposition better match the singer’s vocal capabilities?” (p. 600)
- Barrier to use: “The main barrier to their adoption is, of course, speed.” (p. 600)
- Duration weighting discounts outliers: “it should be noted that 785 Hz is an outlying pitch that is voiced for less than one second, and that the nearest voiced pitch of significant duration is 698 Hz, F5.” (p. 602)
- Duration weighting finds the working zone: “fully half of the piece’s phonation time occurs within the interval of a major third that sits comfortably in the range of all voice types.” (pp. 602 to 603)
- Limit of an aggregate histogram: “If early versions of Tessa showed that 40% of phonation occurred above a certain F0, for example, it was difficult to discern whether this phonation time was dispersed across the entire piece or concentrated into 1 or 2 passages.” (p. 606)
- Not a standalone judgment: “tessiturograms may help guide singers toward vocal repertoire that will challenge, but not strain, their current capabilities.” (p. 606), said “in conjunction with the other estimates of vocal fold vibratory dose [...] and a teacher’s expertise regarding performance practices.”

### Nix (2014)

- History: “The primary shortcoming of this research was its failure to include a durational element for each sung pitch.” (p. 563, on Thurmer's tessiturogram)
- Claimed benefit: “The VRP, tessituragrams, and dosimetry deserve recognition as helpful tools for repertoire selection for teachers of singing and their students.” (p. 571)
- Findings for the one song: “the singer would still have a substantial dynamic range ‘reserve’ to draw upon for expressive usage in performance, indicating that the song should be at least physiologically a comfortable choice for this singer.” (p. 566). And: “Contrary to expectations [...] the performance of the song exceeded the values recorded in the VRP, especially from 370 Hz to 740 Hz.” (p. 568)
- What a duration-weighted tessituragram does not show: “While a tessituragram using Titze’s method indicates where the preponderance of the singing occurs, it does not necessarily indicate what kind of singing is occurring. It is possible that a florid high piece by Rossini and a soaring, legato high piece by Strauss might have similar tessituragrams in terms of cumulative time and cycle doses for each extreme note.” (p. 571)
- Also not captured: phrase direction and the approach to extreme notes (p. 570). “Examining the music score with the singer’s strengths or preferences in mind would likely be preferable and more practical to any type of quantifying process.” (p. 570)

### Elliott (2004)

Elliott does not quantify. She argues the term lacks a duration rule.

- “questions arose that none of the foregoing definitions could address: 1) Which pitch or pitches form the tessitura? 2) How many pitches make a tessitura? 3) What duration of time does sound need ‘enframing’ before it is a tessitura?” (p. 240)
- “Moreover, the occurrence of a single pitch of some duration seems to constitute a unique instance of tessitura, one that should not be ignored and one that needs further investigation.” (p. 241)
- “Since rules in selecting (or omitting) pitches of the tessitura are nonexistent, and, if amplified definitions can better define or describe the quantification of the tessitura, perhaps the place to begin is to build upon historical definitions that musicians either recognize or accept.” (p. 242)
- A participant on duration-weighted display: “I don’t think it is so much how [many] notes lie in a certain area, but it’s a matter of how much the voice lies in a certain area or is within a certain pattern of notes. If something is a high tessitura, it’s a series of notes sustained in a high area instead of jumping up and down. So I think it probably would be best to look at it on a graph or something like that and see how much of it is low, or where it goes up, and how long it is sustained at a high level, [before] it drops down.” (p. 244)
- Her questionnaire asked: “would counting pitches within the vocal line establish the tessitura?” (p. 243)
- The singer-relative point: “So tessitura as a term has nothing to do with anything until you relate it to the person singing it.” (p. 244)
- Her summary: “Discussions indicated that tessitura may range from one predominant pitch to an interval of several pitches, or it may be a combination of both.” (pp. 247 to 248)

### Willis et al. (2025)

- “This innovative analysis demonstrates the importance of granular temporal analysis of vocal demand tasks.” (p. 8)
- The three measures are phonation and rest duration, F0 changes per second, and dB-per-second change (p. 1). Figure 2's caption says: “Note the differences in voiced durations, rest durations, overall duration, tessitura, and fundamental frequency changes per second of each score.” (p. 5)
- READER'S NOTE: none of the three measures is a pitch-height or time-per-pitch measure; tessitura is shown only in the plotted F0 traces.
- “Scores that may seem by ear alone to fit into one category (aerobic vs anaerobic vs mixed bioenergetic profile) may not actually fit into that category upon deeper analysis of the vocal demands.” (p. 8)

### Gaskill et al. (2013)

- A dosimeter returns averages or windows, not a per-pitch histogram: “an average value (or range of values) for frequency and intensity can be obtained for the entire elapsed monitoring time or for any time window of interest.” (p. 12)

### Other papers on Q2

- Titze et al. (2003): nothing on tessitura or per-pitch distribution beyond the speech range profile.
- Sandage and Hoch (2019), Treinkman and Johns (2022), Titze (1983 x3), Titze (2009): nothing.

## Question 3. Tessitura fatigue: a song too high or too low

Direct evidence that a song which lies too high (or too low) for a singer produces fatigue, slower recovery, or harm is thin in these papers. What exists is principle, hypothesis, and self-report.

### Titze (1983), three short columns in J Singing (Jan/Feb, Sept/Oct, Nov/Dec)

- Signs: “Auditorily, we perceive loss of intensity in the extremes of the pitch range. High notes and low notes become weaker, whereas the middle range is preserved a bit longer.” (Part I, Sept/Oct 1983, p. 33)
- “The loss of high notes and loud notes is expected if the parallelism with athletics holds up, but the loss of low notes and soft notes is a bit puzzling. Surely a fatigued weight-lifter can lift his lightest weight and a fatigued runner can walk without much trouble. Apparently singing soft and low involves more than simply reduced muscular effort.” (Part I, p. 33)
- High and loud: “It is no wonder, then, that loud high notes pose special problems with vocal fatigue.” (Part II, Nov/Dec 1983, p. 29). And: “the energy dissipation goes up drastically with higher pitches and loud production, providing some evidence for the fact that singers fatigue easily on prolonged high notes.” (Part II, p. 30)
- Mismatch of task and trained mode: “A larynx may tire easily if a new performance task requires control strategies and structural characteristics that are inconsistent with those that it considered optimal in the developmental stage. Adaptation may be slow, or perhaps impossible in some situations.” (Jan/Feb 1983, p. 22)
- Individual variability: “some teachers and clinicians have adopted the labels tender and robust to differentiate these voice characteristics.” (Jan/Feb 1983, p. 22)
- State of knowledge then: “Research activities in the above areas and in the general area of vocal fatigue have been virtually non-existent.” (Jan/Feb 1983, p. 22)

### Titze et al. (2003): a later reversal on the heat mechanism

- “These results suggest that heat dissipated in the vocal folds probably does not play a very important role in tissue damage.” (PDF p. 14)
- The mechanism preferred in 2003 is acceleration and collision (quoted under Q1, PDF p. 15).

READER'S NOTE: the 1983 high-note argument rests on frictional heating and dissipation; the 2003 paper, with the same first author, downgrades that mechanism. The 2003 paper still states: “Note that the dissipated power is proportional to amplitude squared and frequency squared, inversely proportional to vocal fold thickness squared, and directly proportional to tissue viscosity.” (PDF p. 5).

### Titze (2008)

- Mismatch within range: “The pitches are all able to be sung by the singer, and the overall intensity on the high pitches may be acceptable, but the aria may lack luster due to restricted dynamics. Also, the singer may wear out (fatigue) because he is not able to take the pressure off and ‘coast’ on some of the repeated long duration notes with lower dynamics.” (p. 60)

### Titze (2009)

- Definition: “We define vocal fatigue as the diminished vocal ability (or perception of such by the vocal performer) when effort remains constant. Conversely, it is the increase in effort required to maintain the same vocal ability.” (p. 449)
- Recovery unknown: “The most confounding problem in dealing with vocal fatigue is not knowing how much voice rest is needed for recovery.” (p. 449). And: “What is currently a mystery is the speed at which a redistribution of fluids or a disruption of nutrient and waste product transportation can be reset to normal. It could be as little as a few minutes or as much as several hours. In addition, repair of damaged protein tissue structure can take several days.” (p. 450)
- Test of recovery: “For the moment, try using soft phonation at high pitches as your barometer. If soft and high voice is difficult, especially the day after a strenuous workout, you probably have not fully recovered.” (p. 450)
- Cause: “Faulty technique likely will accelerate the onset of fatigue, but may not be the primary cause.” (p. 449)

### Apfelbach (2020)

- “In addition to the dramatic, artistic, and critical risks posed by a mismatch between singer and tessitura, singing at volumes or in pitch ranges not suited to the voice’s natural capabilities also carries a vocal risk. Although the relationship between vocal demand, the singer’s vocal demand response, and the risk of phonotraumatic injury is likely unique to the singer—and dependent on a host of difficult-to-quantify physiological, psychological, biomechanical, and environmental factors—tessitura and habitual loudness have long been accepted as contributing factors in the development of phonotraumatic lesions such as vocal fold nodules or hemorrhages.” (p. 599)
- Pacing across a long work: “Singers performing the song cycle in full may note the preponderance of high-tessitura songs in the first twelve pieces and apportion their vocal resources accordingly” (p. 603)

### Treinkman and Johns (2022): the main empirical item, women only

- Sample and finding: “Notably, 192 participants (64.9%) experienced an impact on the lower middle range (C4-F4) and/or the lowest range (below C4) as a result of vocal fatigue.” (p. 692); 19.6% reported the higher range (p. 691).
- The question wording: “After extensive voice use and/or a lot of high tessitura singing, many singers notice temporary, often subtle changes in their voices.” (Table 1, p. 690)
- Expectation reversed: “It may be common for clinicians to assume that deficits in the high range might be the best indicator of vocal fatigue.” (p. 689)
- Participant report: “After singing in a high tessitura for a prolonged period, I find it difficult to maintain a low larynx position.” (p. 693)
- Hypothesis, labelled as such by the authors: “If the larynx becomes accustomed to an elevated position, due to high-lying tessitura or tension that can arise from a heavy vocal load, it could impact the ability of the larynx to descend for the lowest pitches. This topic merits further research.” (p. 693)
- Recovery (one subject): “The subject reported significantly improved condition of the lower head voice range after two to three hours of vocal rest. Complete recovery and a return to baseline was achieved by the next day, which corresponds with the experience of many of the participants in the survey.” (p. 693)
- Programming advice from respondents: “Thus, it may be prudent to program higher range repertoire later in a recital program.” (p. 694)
- Limits: “Naturally, conclusions cannot be drawn from a study of one.” (p. 693). “The questionnaire did not ask singers how often they experienced vocal fatigue or after how much singing participants typically experienced the effects of fatigue.” (p. 695). “Vocal ranges of male operatic singers differ greatly from female singers, making a comparison difficult.” (p. 695)

READER'S NOTE: the survey asks about "extensive voice use and/or a lot of high tessitura singing" together; it does not measure the tessitura of what respondents sang. It therefore cannot show that a high-lying song causes low-range change. It reports where fatigue is felt.

### Nix (2014) and Gaskill et al. (2013): timing of the effect

- Nix, describing Carroll et al. (2006): vocal loading coincided with harsher voice-quality ratings “on the day of the load and between 24–72 hours afterward” (p. 562).
- Gaskill: “Other researchers have suggested there is often a 24–72 hour lag in the development of vocal tissue fatigue that could explain these findings.” (p. 17)

### Sandage and Hoch (2019)

- Two kinds of fatigue: “Perceived fatigability can arise when individuals experience fatigue because they have been singing for a long time; the muscles and singing subsystems may not actually be fatigued at the tissue level.” (p. 395)
- Model choice: “Previous models of voice fatigue have focused overwhelmingly on a theoretical model of cover fatigue; however, the physiological up and down regulation of the cover tissue in response to vocal dose has not yet been described to the same extent as muscle tissue mechanisms.” (p. 396)

### Willis et al. (2025)

- Pitch-level demand is flagged as unsettled: “From a bioenergetic perspective in song context, what is less clearly delineated is the potential metabolic demand for scores that require a high tessitura or production of extended high frequencies at lower sound levels, both of which would require significant subsystem muscular contributions.” (p. 8)
- Low and loud: “It could also be hypothesized that higher sound-level production of lower frequencies, which would require more subglottal pressure and therefore more power, may be as metabolically demanding on subsystem skeletal muscle as producing high frequencies softly given that subglottal pressures increase with fundamental frequency increases.” (p. 8)
- Singer-relative: “If a particular piece tends to spend a lot of time in the extreme parts of a singer’s range, this may be more demanding.” (p. 9)

### Elliott (2004): what the term means to musicians

- “For a specific voice Fach: that area of a particular voice where it is most comfortable to sing with endurance and where the brilliant or powerful carrying quality of the voice is most obvious.” (p. 244; a participant's definition)
- “Because the best parts of the voice vary from singer to singer, identification of the pitches of their tessitura will necessarily vary as well.” (p. 248)

### Low voices and too-low songs

- Nothing in the thirteen papers reports fatigue from a song lying too low for a bass or bass-baritone. Treinkman covers female opera singers' low range after extended singing; Sandage and Hoch's participant is “the highly trained baritone” (p. 396) but the study concerns recital training, not tessitura. Titze et al. (2003) lists speaking frequencies for basses, baritones, and tenors (98, 123, and 165 Hz) in a figure caption only (PDF p. 6).

## Question 4. The owner's claim

Claim: “applying well-intentioned pedagogical interventions to accommodate a piece written in a way that taxes the singer's resilience is not optimal for performance.”

READER'S NOTE on the claim: it can be read two ways. (a) Workarounds such as pacing, extra rest, or technical fixes are worse than choosing a piece, role, or key that fits. (b) Training the singer up for a demanding piece is worse than leaving the piece alone. The papers bear on these differently, so I mark which reading each item touches. No paper compares performance quality with and without such interventions (see "Could not establish").

### Support, in the authors' words

Toward reading (a), selection and fit:

- Apfelbach: “an enduring goal of vocal pedagogy has been to match the vocal capabilities of singers with the vocal demands associated with different tessituras” (p. 599). Also “many voice teachers would hesitate to assign a piece with a jarring singer-tessitura mismatch to one of their students.” (p. 600)
- Titze (2008): mismatch costs performance as well as endurance: “the aria may lack luster due to restricted dynamics. Also, the singer may wear out (fatigue)” (p. 60). And a remedy by key change alone is called incomplete: “What is too often done is simply to change the key signature of the song, but unless the accumulated duration and cycle doses are quantified on various pitches within the song, singer-song adaptation still may not be complete.” (p. 60)
- Titze (1983, Part I): “The preferred solution, however, would be to eliminate unnecessary beating of the voice, to educate ourselves to the fact that there is a limit to the mechanical stress that can be placed on the vocal system.” (p. 33)
- Titze (2009): technique is not the whole cause. “Faulty technique likely will accelerate the onset of fatigue, but may not be the primary cause.” (p. 449)
- Treinkman and Johns (respondents): “Several participants wrote about the importance of choosing roles and repertoire carefully, as a preventative measure.” (p. 694)
- Nix: “Astute repertoire selection can help address the technical and musical development of a singer” (p. 561). And: “The VRP, tessituragrams, and dosimetry deserve recognition as helpful tools for repertoire selection” (p. 571).
- Willis et al.: “For performances, the repertoire selected and the order in which they are performed could also be based on the type of vocal demand of each piece, balanced with artistic choices in the order of repertoire.” (p. 8)
- Elliott: singers reported altering repertoire when the tessitura is wrong: “Singers often agreed that their repertoire would be adversely affected or would have to be altered or reconsidered should: (a) the dynamic level at which the pitches of the tessitura be too loud or too soft” (p. 246)

Toward the idea that an accommodation can itself carry a cost:

- Willis et al., on warm-up as an intervention: “There should be no expectation that the warm-up would mitigate fatigue from a metabolic perspective, given that warm-up + performance should be considered in the entire vocal demand metabolic expectation.” (p. 8)
- Treinkman and Johns, same point from singers: “Executing an extended warm-up with a lot of high notes may not be helpful for many singers, as the warm-up becomes a portion of the entire vocal load.” (p. 694)

### Contradiction or qualification, in the authors' words

Toward reading (b), training up is proposed and is not called suboptimal:

- Sandage and Hoch: “In the context of singing training, fatigue is often perceived as something to be avoided. Further, if any vocal fatigue is experienced, the singer often may choose to reduce practice or observe vocal rest with the hope of restoring more optimal voice function. Thus, development of fatigue resistance is a little considered aspect of recital preparation.” (p. 395). Their conclusion: “Lower perceived vocal fatigue across recital trials provides preliminary evidence of a conditioning training effect to meet the demands of performance of Die schöne Müllerin specifically and recital performance in general.” (p. 399)
- Sandage and Hoch's own limits: “Descriptive statistics were used in this preliminary study of voice fatigue in recital preparation and performance.” (p. 397). “Perceived phonatory effort (PPE), as measured along a 100 mm line, did not change more than a few points across the four trials” (p. 397). One singer, no control, and the schedule itself contained accommodations: “For this repertoire, avoid singing it two days in a row; mental practice on day after full run-through; limited studio teaching the day of performance.” (Table 1, p. 396)
- Willis et al.: “Understanding the vocal demands of a piece means that a targeted training regimen can be developed in preparation for performance to upregulate the bioenergetic and metabolic needs to avoid injury and promote fatigue management and recovery.” (p. 8). Also: “If the goal is to frame voice training and conditioning within a context of training up for a known vocal demand, then a reasonable place to start is with score analysis.” (p. 2). They add: “Limited work has been done to develop theoretically grounded training and conditioning programs for the vocal demand tasks of singers/vocal athletes.” (p. 1)
- Apfelbach: a mismatch is not always to be avoided: “A piece whose tessitura lies slightly outside of a singer’s comfortable range may be an excellent growth exercise, yet many voice teachers would hesitate to assign a piece with a jarring singer-tessitura mismatch to one of their students.” (p. 600). And pacing is offered as a tool: tessitura analysis can “determine optimal pacing strategies for lengthy performances.” (p. 603)
- Treinkman and Johns (practice among professionals, no outcome data): “54.4% reported that they tried not to push their voices to be louder and 52.4% reported using semi-occluded vocal tract exercises to recover vocal function.” (p. 692). “A number of singers also expressed the need to prepare for the effects of fatigue by pacing themselves during performance.” (p. 694). Training is said to help: “with training delaying or preventing the onset and symptoms of vocal fatigue.” (p. 688)
- Elliott (practice among professionals): “A complete surprise to me was the practice of ‘trading pitches.’ This occurs among singers who are colleagues, or who know one another well enough to cover pitches within the other’s ‘higher or lower register,’ whenever the voice is not in good shape.” (p. 246). “Most of them stressed the need for versatility but acknowledged there was little need to compromise their vocal health.” (p. 246)
- Titze (1983, Jan/Feb): the 1983 view was that training and adaptation were open questions, not settled against. “Do the vocalis and cricothyroid muscles grow with vocal exercise? We don’t know.” (p. 22). Also, on loudness range rather than pitch range: “Unless different modes of operation are prescribed over various regions of such a large dynamic range, the operation is likely to be inefficient at one of these regions.” (p. 22)
- Nix: the aim is a managed challenge, not zero challenge. A teacher could “more precisely select pieces for the student with the optimal ratio of technical challenges to technical strengths.” (p. 570)
- Gaskill: dose by itself does not predict harm. “individuals can accumulate large vibrational doses in a relatively short period of time and sustain this over the course of a day of teaching or performing, and repeat this for weeks at a time without adverse outcomes.” (p. 12)
- Titze (2008), p. 60 (quoted above), assumes that adapting song to singer by key change is a practice in use. READER'S NOTE: the sentence criticizes that practice as incomplete without per-pitch doses; it does not say adaptation is mistaken.

### Not established for Q4

- No paper tests whether an accommodation (rest, pacing, transposition, warm-up change, training-up programme) lowers or raises performance quality for a piece that taxes a singer.
- Sandage and Hoch and Willis et al. propose training as a way to meet a known demand; neither measures performance quality.
- Treinkman and Johns do not ask whether accommodations helped, only which ones singers use.

## Could not establish

1. Any statement about phonation time per vowel (Q2). Not measured or recommended in any of the thirteen papers. Nix (p. 570) proposes a written list of vowels on the highest and lowest notes, and Elliott (p. 245) records a singer saying vowels change how tessitura feels.
2. A normative dose for singers by repertoire or voice type. Gaskill (pp. 12 to 13) says none exist. The figures that appear (520 m, 3500 m, 7000 m, 3098 m recital, 5403 m student daily mean) come from different devices, populations, and definitions.
3. A validated safe or fatiguing cycle dose for singing. The only limit (520 m distance dose) is borrowed from hand-tool standards and described as unusually stringent.
4. Recovery time after a high-lying or low-lying song. Titze (2009, p. 450) says the speed is a mystery; Treinkman and Johns report next-day recovery for one subject; Nix and Gaskill cite a 24 to 72 hour lag from Carroll et al.
5. Fatigue from a song lying too low, and any data on bass or bass-baritone voices (Q3). The one tessitura-fatigue survey is of women (Treinkman and Johns, p. 695, name male singers as future work). Note that Titze's 2003 data and Gaskill's data include men, but are speech and mixed daily use.
6. Direct evidence that a high-lying song causes more fatigue than a mid-lying one at equal dose. Titze (1983) argues it from mechanics, Apfelbach and Willis state it as accepted or hypothesized, Treinkman and Johns report survey self-report with no measure of what was sung.
7. Whether any accommodation reduces performance quality (Q4). No paper compares.
8. The numeric content of Figures 7 to 9 in Titze et al. (2003), and all equations: absent from the extraction.
9. An independent check of the cycle counts in Titze (2008). See the READER'S NOTE under Q1: the printed counts appear to use whole-note counts in place of seconds.
