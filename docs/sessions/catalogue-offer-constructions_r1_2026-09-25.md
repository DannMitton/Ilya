# Catalogue of offer constructions for autonomy-preserving suggestion language

Purpose: a reference set of phrasing patterns, drawn from fields outside voice pedagogy, that offer guidance while leaving the listener free to decline. For Ilya's English and French suggestion lines. Construction names are mine; patterns and examples are original, written for a singing context. Nothing here is a sentence copied from a source.

Fields covered: motivational interviewing and shared decision-making (healthcare), self-determination theory in education, motor learning and coaching cueing, the Alexander Technique, and (briefly) music teaching and plain-language guidance. Sales and compliance techniques that steer without awareness (dark patterns, embedded commands, NLP-style presupposition, scarcity, foot-in-the-door) were excluded on purpose, per brief, and are not catalogued below even where a source recommends them.

---

## Construction types

### 1. Conditional permission opener
**Pattern:** "If it suits you, ___" / "If that's useful, you might ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- If it suits your voice today, you might let the jaw soften on the approach to that high note.
- If that feels useful, try easing the breath in before the phrase begins.
**French pattern:** « Si cela vous convient, ___ » / « Si cela vous est utile, vous pourriez ___ »
**Examples (FR):** (le [i] est masculin, donc « le garder », « il garde »)
- Si cela vous convient, vous pourriez laisser la mâchoire se détendre en montant vers cette note.
- Si cela vous est utile, essayez de préparer le souffle avant d'attaquer la phrase.
**Fits:** opener.
**Source(s):** Motivational interviewing's elicit-provide-elicit structure builds permission-seeking into information sharing before the clinician offers anything: "seeks permission to offer what the practitioner knows." MINT, "Understanding Motivational Interviewing," https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing (fetched; page describes the EPE structure and permission-seeking but gives no verbatim sample sentence). HAS (Haute Autorité de Santé), "Entretien motivationnel," https://www.has-sante.fr/jcms/p_3501842/fr/entretien-motivationnel (fetched; French source names "demander la permission" as a step but supplies no example wording — snippet only for phrasing, principle confirmed).
**Grounding:** Research-supported as a technique (permission-asking is a defined, studied MI skill), though the specific sentence frame above is my construction, not a tested script.

### 2. "Could" over "should"
**Pattern:** swap the modal of obligation for the modal of possibility: "you could ___" rather than "you should ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- You could let the vowel narrow slightly as you climb, rather than holding it wide.
- You could try starting the phrase a touch softer than feels natural.
**French pattern:** « vous pourriez ___ » plutôt que « vous devriez ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Vous pourriez laisser la voyelle se resserrer légèrement en montant, plutôt que de la garder large.
- Vous pourriez essayer d'entamer la phrase un peu plus doucement que d'instinct.
**Fits:** opener, or the verb inside any opener.
**Source(s):** Altendorf, van Weert, Hoving & Smit, "Should or could? Testing the use of autonomy-supportive language and the provision of choice in online computer-tailored alcohol reduction communication," Digital Health, 2019, https://doi.org/10.1177/2055207619832767 and PMC copy https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6393822/ (title and abstract only located; full text blocked by a verification page on fetch, so treat as snippet only — the study explicitly tests "should" versus "could" phrasing for autonomy support in health messaging, which is exactly this construction, but I could not confirm the direction or size of the effect from the source itself).
**Grounding:** Directly studied contrast in at least one health-communication trial (title/framing confirmed; results NOT ESTABLISHED from what I could open). Consistent with SDT's general distinction between informational and controlling language (see #5).

### 3. Judgment-deferring hedge
**Pattern:** "use your own judgment about ___" / "however suits you"
**Examples (EN):**
- Use your own judgment about how much space to give that vowel here.
- Take whatever breath timing suits you before the leap.
**French pattern:** « selon votre propre jugement » / « comme cela vous convient »
**Examples (FR):**
- Laissez-vous guider par votre propre jugement quant à l'espace à donner à cette voyelle.
- Prenez le temps de respiration qui vous convient avant le saut.
**Fits:** closer, or a standalone frame around the whole suggestion.
**Source(s):** A university-syllabus study coded "please use your best judgment when using your laptop" as autonomy-supportive, contrasted with a controlling rule ("students will be asked to leave class"). Fitó-Bertran et al. (or similar), "Beyond words: the relevance of autonomy-supportive language in university syllabi," Frontiers in Psychology, 2025, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1536821/full (fetched; Table 1 gives this exact phrase as a coded example of autonomy-supportive syllabus language).
**Grounding:** Empirically coded as autonomy-supportive in a peer-reviewed content analysis (not an outcome study of learning gains, but a validated coding scheme).

### 4. Rationale-attached request
**Pattern:** "To [shared goal], you may [action] — whichever way you like."
**Examples (EN):**
- To keep the line even through the passaggio, you may lighten the tone here, in whichever way feels most natural to you.
- To protect the top of the range, you might ease off the volume slightly, however that ease happens for you.
**French pattern:** « Pour [objectif partagé], vous pouvez ___, à votre façon. »
**Examples (FR):**
- Pour garder la ligne égale dans le passage, vous pouvez alléger le son ici, à votre façon.
- Pour préserver l'aigu, vous pourriez réduire un peu le volume, comme cela vous convient.
**Fits:** opener (states the "why" before the "what").
**Source(s):** Reeve, J., "Why Teachers Adopt a Controlling Motivating Style Toward Students and How They Can Become More Autonomy Supportive," Educational Psychologist, 2009, PDF at https://www.csuchico.edu/freespeech/_assets/documents/pedagogy/reeve,-j-2009.pdf (fetched; Table 4 gives the worked example "To help you write your best possible paper, you may work in the way you wish — by yourself or with a partner," offered as a revision of a controlling instruction). My examples follow the same rationale-then-latitude shape but are original wording for singing.
**Grounding:** Strong. Reeve's autonomy-supportive/controlling distinction is a well-cited, empirically grounded line of SDT research in education (Reeve has run classroom-intervention studies training teachers in this style).

### 5. Informational reframe of a rule
**Pattern:** state what the situation calls for, not what the person must do: "This passage tends to want ___" rather than "You must ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- This passage tends to want a little more air support than the one before it.
- A held high note like this often benefits from a slightly narrower vowel.
**French pattern:** « Ce passage demande généralement ___ » plutôt que « Vous devez ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Ce passage demande généralement un peu plus de soutien qu'avant.
- Une note aiguë tenue comme celle-ci profite souvent d'une voyelle légèrement resserrée.
**Fits:** frame for the diagnostic half of the comment, before the suggestion.
**Source(s):** Reeve 2009 (as above) contrasts controlling language like "Quit your complaining and just get the work done" with flexible, informational, nonevaluative messaging. Deci & Ryan's broader self-determination theory literature distinguishes "informational" from "controlling" communication styles; Reeve's paper is the applied classroom source I verified directly.
**Grounding:** Well-grounded in SDT; the informational-versus-controlling distinction is central to the theory and tested across many domains (not specific to singing).

### 6. External-focus cue (outcome, not body part)
**Pattern:** name what happens in the world or in the sound, not what a body part should do: "Focus on ___ [outcome]" rather than "Tighten/relax your ___ [muscle]"
**Examples (EN):**
- Focus on sending the sound toward the back of the hall.
- Aim the vowel at a point just above the eyebrows.
**French pattern:** « Concentrez-vous sur ___ [résultat] » plutôt que sur une partie du corps.
**Examples (FR):**
- Concentrez-vous sur l'idée d'envoyer le son vers le fond de la salle.
- Visez la voyelle vers un point juste au-dessus des sourcils.
**Fits:** the action clause inside an opener, or a closer's image.
**Source(s):** Gabriele Wulf's external-versus-internal focus of attention research in motor learning; summarized with a worked example ("Focus on explosively pushing the bar to the ceiling") at IDEA Health & Fitness Association, "Sample External Cues," https://www.ideafit.com/sample-external-cues/ (fetched). Primary literature: Wulf, G., "Attentional focus and motor learning: a review of 15 years," International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 2013 (not opened directly; referenced by title only — treat the primary citation as snippet-level, the IDEA summary as fetched).
**Grounding:** Strong and specific: a large body of motor-learning research (Wulf and colleagues) finds external-focus instructions improve movement outcomes and automaticity compared with internal-focus (body-part) instructions. This is one of the best-evidenced constructions in the whole catalogue, though the evidence is about learning efficacy, not autonomy per se.

### 7. "Allow" construction (permissive verb, not directive verb)
**Pattern:** "Allow [body part] to ___" rather than "[Body part], do ___" or "Make [body part] ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- Allow the throat to stay open as the phrase rises.
- Allow the ribs to widen on the inhale, rather than lifting them.
**French pattern:** « Laissez ___ » ou « Permettez à ___ de ___ » plutôt qu'un impératif direct sur le corps.
**Examples (FR):**
- Laissez la gorge rester ouverte pendant la montée de la phrase.
- Laissez les côtes s'élargir à l'inspiration, plutôt que de les soulever.
**Fits:** the verb inside the gerund clause (fits directly into Ilya's existing openers, e.g. "Consider letting...").
**Source(s):** F. M. Alexander's directions, as summarized at Hilary King, "Alexander Technique Directions," https://www.hilaryking.net/alexander-technique/directions (fetched; gives the four canonical directions built on "allow," e.g. "Allow my neck be free," "Allow my head to go forward and up," and states directions describe a direction of movement rather than a fixed end position, avoiding prescriptive muscular commands).
**Grounding:** This is the construction Ilya already uses most (its favourite is a variant of it). Grounding is in long-standing pedagogical practice (over a century of Alexander Technique teaching) rather than controlled outcome research on the language itself; the technique's physiological claims have some empirical support, but the linguistic choice of "allow" specifically has not been isolated and tested the way Wulf's external-focus cues have. Best described as strong practice-based grounding, not experimental grounding.

### 8. Directional, not positional, phrasing
**Pattern:** describe a direction of change, not a fixed target state: "let ___ move toward ___" rather than "put ___ in ___ position"
**Examples (EN):**
- Let the breath keep moving outward through the whole phrase, rather than settling.
- Let the sound keep travelling forward as you descend.
**French pattern:** « Laissez ___ continuer vers ___ » plutôt qu'une position fixe.
**Examples (FR):**
- Laissez le souffle continuer de se déployer tout au long de la phrase, sans se figer.
- Laissez le son continuer d'avancer pendant que vous descendez.
**Fits:** closer, especially where the suggestion is about sustaining a quality through a passage rather than hitting a single moment.
**Source(s):** Same Hilary King page as #7: "There is no such thing as a right position, but there is such a thing as a right direction," attributed to Alexander Technique teaching.
**Grounding:** Practice-based (see #7); no controlled study of this phrasing isolated from the rest of the technique was located.

### 9. Evocative question (open, not rhetorical)
**Pattern:** a genuine open question the singer answers to themselves: "What happens if you ___?" / "What do you notice when ___?"
**Examples (EN):**
- What happens if you let the vowel darken slightly on the way up?
- What do you notice in your breath when you take that entrance a beat later?
**French pattern:** « Que se passe-t-il si vous ___? » / « Que remarquez-vous quand ___? »
**Examples (FR):**
- Que se passe-t-il si vous laissez la voyelle s'assombrir légèrement en montant?
- Que remarquez-vous dans votre souffle quand vous prenez cette entrée un temps plus tard?
**Fits:** closer (this is Ilya's own favourite closer family, "and notice whether...", in question form).
**Source(s):** MI's "evocative questions," MINT, https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing (fetched; states evocative questions "guide the client to reflect on how change may be meaningful or possible," letting the client generate their own reasons rather than receiving prescribed ones — principle confirmed, no verbatim example given).
**Grounding:** MI's evocative-question technique has a substantial outcomes literature behind the broader MI method (behaviour-change trials across health domains), though the literature evaluates MI as a whole conversational method more than this phrasing pattern in isolation.

### 10. Self-experiment framing
**Pattern:** cast the suggestion as a trial the singer runs on themselves, with an observable result: "Try ___ and notice whether ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- Try letting the tongue relax on that syllable and notice whether the tone opens up.
- Try starting the crescendo a bar early and notice whether the climax feels easier to reach.
**French pattern:** « Essayez de ___ et remarquez si ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Essayez de laisser la langue se détendre sur cette syllabe et remarquez si le timbre s'ouvre.
- Essayez de commencer le crescendo une mesure plus tôt et remarquez si le sommet devient plus facile à atteindre.
**Fits:** the full opener-plus-closer unit; this is already Ilya's favourite construction and matches the "experiment the singer judges" brief exactly.
**Source(s):** This shape combines MI's evocative questioning (#9) with the coaching literature's emphasis on the learner evaluating their own movement outcome rather than being told the verdict (see Wulf, #6, on the learner attending to an external outcome). No single source states this exact combined construction; it is a synthesis, flagged as such rather than attributed to one paper.
**Grounding:** Composite construction; each half is separately grounded (see #6, #9), but the fusion itself is not something I found named or tested as a unit. Practice-level grounding: it is a common shape in coaching and teaching generally, but I could not find a source studying "try X, notice Y" as its own construction.

### 11. Explicit menu of options
**Pattern:** offer two or more concrete alternatives and let the person pick, rather than one prescribed action: "You could either ___, or ___ — whichever feels closer to what you want."
**Examples (EN):**
- You could either taper the final note, or hold it steady to the cutoff — whichever suits the line you're after.
- You could soften the consonant, or keep it crisp and pull back the volume instead.
**French pattern:** « Vous pourriez soit ___, soit ___ — selon ce qui vous convient. »
**Examples (FR):**
- Vous pourriez soit estomper la dernière note, soit la tenir fermement jusqu'à la coupure, selon la ligne que vous recherchez.
- Vous pourriez soit adoucir la consonne, soit la garder nette en réduisant plutôt le volume.
**Fits:** opener, when the underlying pedagogy genuinely supports more than one solution.
**Source(s):** Shared decision-making literature frames choice-offering as a core patient-centred skill; overview at NCBI Bookshelf, "Patient-Centered Communication and Shared Decision Making," https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK202146/ (title/summary level; not opened in full, snippet only). The SHARE Approach (AHRQ/University of Colorado), https://medschool.cuanschutz.edu/docs/librariesprovider294/default-document-library/SHARE2_final.pdf, names "Explore the Options" as one of five shared-decision-making steps (snippet only, not fetched in full).
**Grounding:** Shared decision-making as a whole has a substantial evidence base in healthcare (improves patient knowledge and satisfaction, per multiple systematic reviews); the specific two-option sentence frame above is my construction, following that principle rather than a tested script.

### 12. Named-authority offer
**Pattern:** attribute the suggestion to a source, softening it from the speaker's personal command to a documented possibility: "[Author/tradition] suggests ___" / "In [X]'s teaching, singers sometimes ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- In the bel canto tradition, teachers sometimes suggest narrowing the vowel here to protect the top.
- Miller notes that some singers find it easier to release tension by imagining the sound continuing past the note.
**French pattern:** « Selon [auteur/tradition], on suggère parfois ___ » / « Dans l'enseignement de [X], certains chanteurs ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Dans la tradition du bel canto, on suggère parfois de resserrer la voyelle ici pour protéger l'aigu.
- Miller note que certains chanteurs trouvent plus facile de relâcher la tension en imaginant le son se prolonger au-delà de la note.
**Fits:** opener (Ilya's existing "[Author] suggests letting..." already belongs to this family).
**Grounding:** Not a technique described in the autonomy-support literature I searched; it functions rhetorically (the suggestion is framed as one documented option among others, not the teacher's personal decree) rather than as a studied psychological mechanism. NOT ESTABLISHED as an autonomy-support construction in the literature; included because it is already load-bearing in Ilya's set and is consistent with the same logic (distancing the suggestion from a command).
**Source(s):** none specific to this construction found; reasoning is mine.

### 13. Provisional/tentative verb framing
**Pattern:** hedge the claim itself, not just the instruction: "This might ___" / "This can sometimes ___" rather than "This will ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- This can sometimes ease the strain you're describing at the top of the phrase.
- Letting the vowel narrow might give the note a little more ring.
**French pattern:** « Cela peut parfois ___ » / « Cela pourrait ___ » plutôt que « Cela va ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Cela peut parfois soulager la tension que vous ressentez au sommet de la phrase.
- Resserrer légèrement la voyelle pourrait donner un peu plus d'éclat à la note.
**Fits:** closer, specifically the outcome clause (this is the "possibility, never a promise" rule made explicit).
**Source(s):** General MI principle that the counsellor avoids asserting outcomes and instead lets the client's own experience confirm or disconfirm them; stated at the level of MI's "spirit" (acceptance, evocation) in MINT's overview, https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing (fetched; general principle, no example sentence).
**Grounding:** Consistent with MI's broader spirit but not a named, separately studied construction; treat as reasoned application of MI principles rather than a directly cited technique.

### 14. Reflective lead-in
**Pattern:** open by naming what the note (or the singer's likely experience) is asking, before offering anything: "This note asks for ___. One way to meet that is ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- This note asks for a long, even breath. One way to meet that is to take the inhale a beat earlier than usual.
- The leap here asks for a clean release beforehand. You might try letting the previous vowel close a touch sooner.
**French pattern:** « Cette note demande ___. Une façon d'y répondre est ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Cette note demande un souffle long et régulier. Une façon d'y répondre est de prendre l'inspiration un temps plus tôt que d'habitude.
- Le saut ici demande une fin de son nette. Vous pourriez laisser la voyelle précédente se refermer un peu plus tôt.
**Fits:** opener, specifically the "what the note asks" half of Ilya's comment structure, bridging into the suggestion.
**Source(s):** MI's reflective listening ("repeating, rephrasing or offering a deeper guess about what the person is trying to communicate") is the closest analogue, applied here to the note's demand rather than a client's statement — MINT, https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing (fetched).
**Grounding:** Adapted from a verified MI technique (reflection) but repurposed for a non-dialogic context (a note has no utterance to reflect); grounding is by analogy, not direct application.

### 15. Invitation to test, not to adopt
**Pattern:** frame the suggestion as worth testing once, not worth keeping: "Worth a try here is ___" / "See how it feels to ___, just for this phrase."
**Examples (EN):**
- Worth a try here is holding the consonant a little longer than usual.
- See how it feels to let the dynamic drop right before the climax, just for this phrase.
**French pattern:** « Cela vaut la peine d'essayer ___ » / « Voyez ce que cela donne de ___, juste pour cette phrase. »
**Examples (FR):**
- Cela vaut la peine d'essayer de tenir la consonne un peu plus longtemps que d'habitude.
- Voyez ce que cela donne de laisser la dynamique redescendre juste avant le sommet, seulement pour cette phrase.
**Fits:** opener; useful where the standard gerund-opener would feel repetitive.
**Source(s):** Consistent with SDT autonomy-support literature's general finding that time-limited, low-commitment framing reduces the sense of being controlled (see Reeve 2009, as above, on "flexible, nonevaluative messages"); this specific phrase shape is mine, not lifted from the paper.
**Grounding:** General principle grounded (Reeve); specific phrasing is original.

### 16. Acknowledgment-first frame
**Pattern:** name a plausible difficulty or preference before offering the suggestion, so the offer reads as responsive rather than generic: "If [difficulty] is what you're finding, one option is ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- If the top feels pinched here, one option is easing the volume back slightly on the approach.
- If breath is running short by the end of the phrase, you might try dividing it with a quick catch breath mid-line.
**French pattern:** « Si vous trouvez que ___, une option est ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Si vous trouvez que l'aigu se serre ici, une option est de réduire légèrement le volume en approchant la note.
- Si le souffle vous manque en fin de phrase, vous pourriez essayer une courte respiration de reprise au milieu de la ligne.
**Fits:** opener, conditional on the diagnostic content already present in Ilya's comment.
**Source(s):** Reflects MI's practice of acknowledging the client's frame before offering information (part of the "elicit" steps of elicit-provide-elicit); MINT, https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing (fetched, general principle only).
**Grounding:** Principle-level grounding via MI; sentence construction is mine.

### 17. Non-imperative instruction via nominalization
**Pattern:** turn the action into a noun phrase offered for consideration, rather than a verb commanding the listener: "One option is a slight narrowing of the vowel here." rather than "Narrow the vowel."
**Examples (EN):**
- One option is a slight lengthening of the breath before this entrance.
- A small softening of the final consonant is worth considering here.
**French pattern:** « Une option est un léger resserrement de la voyelle ici. »
**Examples (FR):**
- Une option est un léger allongement du souffle avant cette entrée.
- Un léger adoucissement de la consonne finale mérite d'être envisagé ici.
**Fits:** opener, as a variant when a gerund clause would be awkward.
**Source(s):** No source specifically studies nominalization as an autonomy-support device; this construction is included because it is grammatically distinct from the gerund-opener family already in Ilya's set and gives translators (see French notes) a way around gerund clauses that read stiffly in French.
**Grounding:** NOT ESTABLISHED in the literature searched; included on structural, not evidential, grounds.

### 18. Plain, unhedged statement of fact plus separate offer
**Pattern:** state the observable fact plainly, then separate the offer from it clearly, so the offer does not get diluted by excessive hedging: "This phrase sits high in your range. One thing worth trying is ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- This phrase sits high in your range. One thing worth trying is starting it a little more covered than you would lower down.
- The line moves quickly through several vowels. One thing worth trying is keeping the jaw still as you move between them.
**French pattern:** « Cette phrase se situe dans l'aigu de votre tessiture. Une chose à essayer est ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Cette phrase se situe dans l'aigu de votre tessiture. Une chose à essayer est de la commencer un peu plus couverte que dans le médium.
- La ligne traverse rapidement plusieurs voyelles. Une chose à essayer est de garder la mâchoire immobile en passant de l'une à l'autre.
**Fits:** frame for the whole two-part comment (fact, then offer); directly answers the brief's warning against hedging into mush.
**Source(s):** Plain-language guidance generally recommends stating facts directly and reserving softening language for genuine matters of choice, so that hedges retain their meaning; general principle found across plain-language style guides (e.g., government plain-language guidance), not tied to one source I verified in full — treat as informed general knowledge, not a specific citation. NOT ESTABLISHED against a specific source for this session.
**Grounding:** Practice-based; no specific study located.

### 19. Third-person generalization softening
**Pattern:** describe what singers in general, or the voice in general, tends to do, leaving the individual singer free to test whether it applies to them: "Some singers find that ___" / "It's common for the voice to ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- Some singers find that a touch more space in the mouth helps this vowel carry.
- It's common for the breath to want a little longer here than the tempo suggests.
**French pattern:** « Certains chanteurs trouvent que ___ » / « Il est courant que la voix ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Certains chanteurs trouvent qu'un peu plus d'espace dans la bouche aide cette voyelle à porter.
- Il est courant que le souffle demande un peu plus de temps ici que ne le suggère le tempo.
**Fits:** opener, as a lead-in before the personal offer, or a standalone soft claim.
**Source(s):** Analogous to MI's normalization technique (stating that a struggle or response is common, to reduce defensiveness) and to shared decision-making's use of population-level evidence framed as "some patients" rather than "you should." General principle, not tied to one verified source in this search — NOT ESTABLISHED against a specific citation.
**Grounding:** Practice-based inference from adjacent, verified techniques (MI normalization, evidence-based patient information); not independently confirmed here.

### 20. Time-boxed trial closer
**Pattern:** limit the suggestion explicitly to the present attempt, underlining that nothing is being asked permanently: "just for this run-through" / "for now, see whether ___"
**Examples (EN):**
- Just for this run-through, try holding back the crescendo until the last two bars.
- For now, see whether a lighter attack on the consonant changes how the phrase lands.
**French pattern:** « juste pour cet essai » / « pour l'instant, voyez si ___ »
**Examples (FR):**
- Juste pour cet essai, retenez le crescendo jusqu'aux deux dernières mesures.
- Pour l'instant, voyez si une attaque plus légère sur la consonne change la façon dont la phrase atterrit.
**Fits:** closer.
**Source(s):** Consistent with SDT's finding that autonomy support is strengthened when a request is framed as low-stakes and reversible rather than a standing rule (see Reeve 2009's syllabus contrast, and the Frontiers syllabi study on time-limited flexible language). Neither source states this exact "time-boxed" construction; it is my synthesis of their general finding.
**Grounding:** Principle-level support; specific construction not directly tested.

## Warnings: constructions that sound autonomy-supportive but are not

**English:**
- **The rhetorical question that is really a command.** "Wouldn't it be better to soften that note?" presents a closed, leading question with only one acceptable answer; it borrows the syntax of an open question (#9) without the substance. Reeve 2009 specifically flags this pattern in education ("A good student would revise her paper, wouldn't she?") as controlling language disguised as a question, tied to conditional approval rather than genuine inquiry.
- **The false choice.** Offering two options that both lead to the same outcome ("You could try it my way now, or my way after you've struggled a bit") is not a real menu (#11); it manufactures the appearance of choice while removing it. Exclude.
- **The buried imperative.** "You might want to just go ahead and narrow that vowel" uses the surface grammar of an offer ("you might") around a verb phrase ("just go ahead and") that cancels the hedge. Watch for softeners that intensify rather than soften.
- **The guilt-adjacent rationale.** "If you really want this to work, you'll need to..." attaches an offer's grammar to a threat about the relationship or the outcome; this is conditional regard, which Reeve's research treats as a controlling strategy, not an autonomy-supportive one, however gently it is phrased.
- **Hedging into vagueness.** Excessive qualification ("this might possibly sometimes help a little, perhaps") removes enough content that the sentence stops being informative; the brief's own caution against "hedged into mush" matches this exactly. Calm authority requires the factual half of the sentence (what the note asks) to stay unhedged; only the recommendation and its outcome should carry the offer-language.

**French:**
- **La question rhétorique.** « Ne pensez-vous pas qu'il vaudrait mieux alléger cette note? » a la forme d'une question ouverte mais n'accepte qu'une réponse ; à éviter pour les mêmes raisons qu'en anglais.
- **Le faux choix.** Proposer deux options qui mènent en réalité au même résultat n'est pas un vrai menu ; à exclure.
- **L'impératif déguisé en offre.** « Vous pourriez tout simplement resserrer cette voyelle » — « tout simplement » annule l'effet du conditionnel « pourriez ».
- **Le conditionnel de politesse vide de sens.** En français, le conditionnel (« vous pourriez », « il faudrait ») sert aussi à la politesse générale et pas seulement à l'offre d'autonomie ; utilisé partout, il perd sa valeur distinctive. Réserver le conditionnel aux moments où un vrai choix existe, et garder l'indicatif pour les faits établis (voir Construction 18), évite que tout le texte sonne pareil.
- **Le sujet vague de responsabilité, « on ».** « On pourrait alléger cette note » dilue qui, au juste, fait l'offre et à qui elle s'adresse ; préférer « vous pourriez » qui nomme clairement le chanteur comme destinataire et décideur.

## French notes

- **Gender agreement with a bare vowel.** In French, a vowel named on its own — « le [i] », « le [u] », « le [a] » — takes the masculine, because the implicit noun is *le son* or *le phonème*. Any pattern that refers back to the vowel with a pronoun or agreement must use masculine forms: « le [i] garde sa couleur » (not *la*), « gardez-le » (not *la*), « le [i] est fermé » (not *fermée*). This matters most in closer constructions like #9 and #10, where the pattern refers back to the vowel just introduced. Check agreement every time a vowel is the grammatical subject or object of the closer's second clause.
- **Gerund openers do not always translate cleanly.** English "Consider letting the jaw soften" uses a gerund object after "letting"; French does not have a direct equivalent construction that reads naturally in formal registre. The idiomatic French equivalent restructures around an infinitive: « Envisagez de laisser la mâchoire se détendre » (infinitive after "envisagez de," not a French gerund). Do not force a French participe présent ("en laissant...") into the opener slot; it reads as a subordinate manner clause, not an offer, and changes the sentence's function.
- **"You might" has no single French equivalent; French spreads the hedge across mood and modal verb rather than one word.** English "might" is carried in French partly by the conditional (« pourriez », « seriez ») and partly by adverbs of possibility (« peut-être », « éventuellement »). Overusing both together (conditional plus adverb) over-hedges and produces the "mush" the brief warns against; pick one mechanism per sentence, not both.
- **Formal "vous" and its effect on directive verbs.** Because "vous" already carries formality and distance in French, direct imperatives in "vous" form ("Essayez de...", "Laissez...") read less commanding to a French ear than the equivalent English imperative would, since French imperative + vous already implies a respectful register. This means French can sometimes use construction #15's imperative-shaped opener ("Essayez...", "Voyez...") where English would need "You might try..." to achieve the same warmth — a straight translation of every English hedge word into French can over-soften and sound less like calm authority, more like uncertainty. When translating, prefer matching the *register* (formal vous, unhurried pacing) over matching every individual hedge word.
- **Canadian French versus European French register.** Sources fetched in this research were mostly European French institutional sources (HAS, French academic PDFs); none were specifically Québécois. The French examples above use vocabulary acceptable in both registers (no anglicisms, no distinctly European colloquialisms), but I did not locate Canadian-specific guidance on autonomy-supportive medical or teaching language, so the Canadian-French naturalness of these examples rests on general knowledge of the register, not a verified Canadian source. Flagged under "What I could not establish."
