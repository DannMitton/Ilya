# Research memo r1: when did -кий, -гий, -хий and -кивать, -гивать, -хивать shift from hard to soft velar?

Date: 2026-11-09. Web research only. Nothing was written to any repository.

Headline: I did not find any source, verified, that dates the shift for these endings by decades, generations, or birth cohorts. I verified one primary source that fixes the hard reading as the Moscow literary norm in 1935, one dissertation abstract that treats the hard velar in -ивать verbs as an Old Moscow feature of the turn of the 19th to 20th century, and two Wikipedia articles (secondary) that point to Avanesov 1984 for the modern soft reading. Panov, Avanesov, Krysin, Comrie et al., Jones and Ward, and Timberlake could not be opened. No source I read ties the change to the date of a text.

## Ranked sources

### 1. Ushakov (ed.), Tolkovyj slovar' russkogo jazyka, vol. 1 (1935), introduction

- Citation: Толковый словарь русского языка / под ред. Д. Н. Ушакова. Т. 1: А–Кюрины. М.: Гос. изд-во иностранных и национальных словарей, 1935. Введение, раздел II «Сведения по правописанию, произношению и грамматике», § 23 «Произношение», пп. 14 и 15.
- Quoted claim, item 14: «Окончания муж. р. ед. ч. прилагательных -гий, -кий, -хий (долгий, широкий, тихий) произносятся так, как если бы было написано гой, кой, хой. Следовательно, одинаково произносятся окончания: «долгий путь» и «после долгой зимы», «широкий ворот» и «у широкой рубахи», «тихий ход» и «у тихой пристани».»
- Gloss: the masculine nominative singular endings -гий, -кий, -хий are pronounced as if spelled -гой, -кой, -хой, so «тихий ход» and «у тихой пристани» end the same way.
- Quoted claim, item 15: «В глаголах на -гивать, -кивать, -хивать (натягивать, постукивать, помахивать) слоги ги, ки, хи произносятся так, как если бы было написано го, ко, хо.»
- Gloss: in verbs in -гивать, -кивать, -хивать the syllables ги, ки, хи are pronounced as if spelled го, ко, хо.
- Framing: § 23 opens with the norms of "литературного произношения", and item 16 on reflexive -сь speaks of the Moscow pronunciation as preferred. So the section describes the Moscow literary norm as Ushakov codified it. Ushakov gives no variant for items 14 and 15 and gives no date, cohort, or generation for them.
- Page: the OCR page header near item 14 reads «ХХХУ». English Wikipedia cites this passage as column XXXIV (Ushakov, vol. 1, 1935, col. XXXIV). The intro is paged in columns. I read OCR text, not the page image, so the exact column is NOT VERIFIED (XXXIV or XXXV).
- Access route: Internet Archive scan, item `tolkovyjslovarrusskogojazykat1akjur55`, OCR full text (`..._djvu.txt`), about line 2841 of the file. The OCR garbled «хой» as «той» and «го, ко, хо» as «20, ко, т0». I corrected both from the sense and from the parallel «гой, кой». The page scan itself was not viewed.
- Verification status: VERIFIED as to text (OCR, two letters corrected). Column NOT VERIFIED.
- Note on the lead: Ushakov says "как если бы было написано кой". That is the reduced vowel of -кой (roughly [kəj]), not [ɨj]. The [ɨj] variant in the brief is not supported by anything I read.

### 2. Antonova, O. V., dissertation abstract, "Система старомосковского произношения и ее рефлексы в современной звучащей речи" (2008)

- Citation: Антонова О. В. Система старомосковского произношения и ее рефлексы в современной звучащей речи: дис. … канд. филол. наук, спец. 10.02.01 «Русский язык». М., 2008. 167 с.
- Quoted claim (introduction of the abstract): «… остается неясным ряд вопросов, связанных с различными фактическими составляющими этого явления, начиная от определения хронологических рамок и заканчивая выяснением социальной принадлежности контингента носителей старой московской нормы.»
- Gloss: the chronological limits, and the social identity of the speakers, of the Old Moscow norm are still unsettled.
- Quoted claim (conclusions): the Old Moscow system is the speech of Muscovites «рубежа XIX — XX веков», and among the reflexes still present in modern speech the abstract lists «произношение в глаголах с основой на заднеязычный согласный, оканчивающихся на -ивать, в конце основы твердого согласного звука».
- Gloss: the hard final velar in verbs with a velar stem ending in -ивать is a reflex of Old Moscow pronunciation still heard in current speech. The abstract also says many Old Moscow variants are now regarded as either «полностью устарелые» (fully obsolete) or «просторечные» (substandard colloquial).
- Table of contents of the dissertation: § 1.2.3.1.1 «Твердость/мягкость заднеязычного согласного в конце основы прилагательных в форме им. п. ед. ч. м. р.» (about pp. 64 to 65) and § 1.2.3.2 «Произношение глаголов с основой на заднеязычный согласный, оканчивающихся на -ивать» (p. 66). The dissertation therefore treats exactly our endings. I could not read those sections, so I do not know what they say about timing.
- Page: none. The abstract text on the page is not paginated.
- Access route: dissercat.com page for the dissertation (abstract and table of contents shown in HTML). The OCR on that page is rough.
- Verification status: abstract text VERIFIED. Dissertation body, pp. 64 to 66, NOT READ. The abstract names no date for the velar shift.

### 3. Avanesov, Russkoe literaturnoe proiznoshenie (1984), pp. 196 to 197 and 208, via English Wikipedia

- Citation: Аванесов Р. И. Русское литературное произношение. 5-е изд. М.: Просвещение, 1984.
- Claim as relayed (English Wikipedia, "Russian phonology"): "In adjectives ending in -кий, -гий, -хий, traditional Moscow norm prescribed the pronunciation [kəj, ɡəj, xəj] (as if spelled -кой, -гой, -хой), but now those adjectives are usually pronounced according to the spelling, thus [kʲɪj, ɡʲɪj, xʲɪj]." It cites Ushakov 1935 col. XXXIV for the old norm and Avanesov 1984 pp. 196 to 197 for the modern one. For the verbs it cites Avanesov 1984 p. 208.
- Whose claim: Wikipedia editors', attributing the modern reading to Avanesov. No date in the sentence beyond "now".
- Access route: raw wikitext of the English Wikipedia article (via the MediaWiki raw action).
- Verification status: Wikipedia text VERIFIED. Avanesov 1984, pp. 196 to 197 and 208: NOT VERIFIED. I did not read the primary, and I do not know what Avanesov says about when or among whom the change happened.

### 4. Russian Wikipedia, "Московское произношение", for the general window

- Claims as relayed: «Образцовое "старомосковское произношение" выработалось в конце XIX — начале XX века и присутствует лишь у небольшого числа в основном пожилых людей; также поддерживается театральной традицией …» (citing БСЭ, "Русский язык"). «В середине XX века сформировалась новая общенациональная произносительная норма …» (citing Verbitskaya, "Варианты русского литературного произношения", gramota.ru, read there by Wikipedia as an archived copy). It also repeats that Ushakov wrote that -гий, -кий, -хий were pronounced -гой, -кой, -хой, and the same for -кивать, -гивать, -хивать.
- Whose claim: Wikipedia, citing the Great Soviet Encyclopedia and Verbitskaya. The primaries are NOT VERIFIED.
- Access route: raw wikitext via the MediaWiki raw action.
- Verification status: secondary only. Useful as the general dating of the whole Old Moscow system (formed late 19th to early 20th century, displaced by a new national norm in the middle of the 20th century). It does not single out the velar endings.

### 5. Worth (1978) rhyme example, via Laletina and Khvorostianova (2023)

- Citation of the secondary: Лалетина О. С., Хворостьянова Е. В. Грамматика рифмы М. В. Ломоносова. SPbU (DOI 10.30842/ielcp2306901528140), p. 2199. Read as a PDF from the SPbU research portal.
- What it says: it quotes Worth's classification of rhyme pairs in Onegin and gives «убогий / строгой» as an example of a Group 2 pair (same part of speech, different grammatical form) (Worth 1978: 775).
- Why it matters: it shows -гий rhyming with -гой in a Pushkin text, which is the kind of evidence the brief mentions. It does not say anything about pronunciation, and it does not claim the rhyme proves a hard velar.
- Primary (Dean Worth, 1978) NOT VERIFIED. I did not locate the passage in Onegin.

## Sources read that did not help

- Kalenchuk, Kasatkina and Kasatkin, article in the Apresjan festschrift (2011), and Kasatkina and Nikolaeva festschrift article (2013): read in full for our endings, nothing found.
- Avanesov and Ozhegov (eds.), Russkoe literaturnoe udarenie i proiznoshenie (1955 scan), and the Avanesov-edited Orfoepicheskij slovar' (4th ed., 1988): searched for the endings and for "старомосковск", "поколени", "заднеязыч". I found the label «удерживается по преимуществу в речи старшего поколения» applied to other features and no statement on -кий endings. I only searched, I did not read these front to back.
- Yanushevskaya and Bunčić, "Russian" (JIPA 2015): says velar plus /i/ sequences are rare; nothing on the shift.
- Russkaja rech' 2004 no. 5, pp. 99 to 100: cites Panov 1990 for a different feature (ск[ры]петь was normative in the 19th century). It shows how Panov's book is used but says nothing about our endings.

## Best citable statement for Ilya's rule

Supported by what I verified:

> In the Moscow literary norm as codified by D. N. Ushakov in 1935, the unstressed endings -гий, -кий, -хий and the syllables ги, ки, хи in -гивать, -кивать, -хивать were pronounced as if spelled -гой, -кой, -хой and -го, -ко, -хо, that is with a hard velar and a reduced vowel (Ушаков 1935, vol. 1, introduction, § 23, items 14 and 15). Later descriptions, relayed by Wikipedia from Avanesov 1984 (primary not verified), give the spelling-based soft reading as the usual modern one. The Old Moscow system as a whole is dated to the late 19th and early 20th century, with a new national norm taking over in the middle of the 20th century (Russian Wikipedia, citing БСЭ and Verbitskaya; secondary).

What this supports for the rule: a window, roughly "hard reading codified as the norm in the 1930s; soft reading usual by the 1980s". It does not support a precise year, a birth cohort, or a text-date cutoff. Every source I found dates norms or speakers, none dates texts. A rule keyed to the date of a poem's words would be Ilya's own inference from these norm dates, not a published finding, and should be labelled that way. The phrase "reduced [ə]" rather than [ɨ] follows Ushakov's «кой».

## Gaps

1. Avanesov 1984 (pp. 196 to 197, 208) and earlier editions: the key primary for the modern norm; unread.
2. Panov 1990 (Nauka; later reprinted 2002): not opened. Antonova's bibliography lists the 2002 edition, and a Russkaja rech' article cites the 1990 edition.
3. Antonova's dissertation body, pp. 64 to 66: the likeliest place to find a dated statement on exactly these endings, with a discussion of Panov, Avanesov, and recordings of Maly Theatre actors of the mid-20th century.
4. Krysin (ed.), Russkij jazyk po dannym massovogo obsledovanija (1974): not found online. Whether it surveyed these endings by birth year is unknown to me.
5. Comrie, Stone and Polinsky (1996) and Jones and Ward (1969): both are on the Internet Archive as lending copies; the in-book search was refused (item not available), so I read nothing from them.
6. Kalenchuk and Kasatkina, Slovar' trudnostej russkogo proiznoshenija (1997): not found online. Timberlake (2004), Kasatkina and Kuz'mina on stage speech, Ushakov 1928: not opened.
7. Ushakov's introduction: column number needs a look at the scan. The Internet Archive PDF is 160 MB; I did not download it.
8. No article in Voprosy jazykoznanija, Russian Linguistics, SEEJ, or JSL on this specific change turned up in my searches.

## What I could not establish

- Any dated statement, by decade, generation, or birth cohort, for the shift in these endings.
- What Avanesov, Panov, Krysin, or Comrie et al. actually say about it.
- Any source linking the shift to the date of a text. The Pushkin rhyme evidence exists as rhyme pairs (убогий / строгой, via Worth), but I found no scholarly statement here interpreting it as pronunciation evidence.
- Whether the Old Moscow reading was [əj] or [ɨj] in practice. Ushakov's wording points to [əj]. The [ɨj] in the brief is not confirmed.
- Popular pages (Mel.fm, a teaching handout on Google Docs) repeat the -кой rule but are not scholarly and I do not cite them.
