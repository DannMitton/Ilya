# Metre transitions: what the sources say, and what Ilya needs

## Header

Dann's hypothesis is refined, not confirmed. Printed signals of a relationship are well attested and are the only fixed point; the sources I could reach state no universal default for any era. The strongest concrete evidence is Beethoven's metronome marks across a change of metre in the String Quartet op. 132, second movement, which hold the bar, not the eighth, constant.

## Answer 1: persistence, courtesy signatures, mixed metres

- A metre holds until another is printed. Gould p. 152 (QUOTE).
- Courtesy (cautionary) signatures are prescribed at a system break, after the last barline. Gould p. 152 (QUOTE). The new signature sits after the barline.
- Mixed metres are a separate form: signatures joined by "+" (fixed) or a hyphen (irregular), both at the outset. Gould p. 179 (QUOTE). In the Gould memo, section P, Gould treats it as more common to read the pattern as single bars divided by dotted barlines.
- Gould's words cover persistence of the metre, not what the tempo does at the change. Persistence of metre is not persistence of pulse.

## Answer 2: printed signals of a relationship

| Signal | Meaning | Instrument and source |
|---|---|---|
| l'istesso tempo | "The same tempo. An indication in a composition that directs that the beat remains constant when the meter changes." Example given: 2/4 to 6/8, "the measure still has two beats, and the beat's speed stays the same. Only the beat's subdivision changes." | QUOTE of the first sentence, paraphrase of the example, from an online dictionary entry (onmusic, updated 2016): https://dev-dictionary.onmusic.org/terms/1901-listesso_tempo. Authorship and edition of the dictionary not established. |
| Note = note equation | "Modulations are usually notated as 'note value = note value,' with the new tempo given in parentheses afterward." | doppio movimento / doppio più lento | "Twice as fast," the new tempo "exactly double the previous one." Called "common in 19th and 20th-century music." | ABSTRACT, commercial glossary (Songtive): https://www.songtive.com/cs/glossary/doppio-movimento. Weak source. |

Dates of common use. Wikipedia (ABSTRACT) says earlier composers used doppio più mosso and più lento, and that l'istesso tempo covered what is now notated as metric modulation. It credits Richard Franko Goldman with naming metric modulation in a review of Carter's Cello Sonata (1948); Carter preferred "tempo modulation." Carter's own earliest printed equation date is NOT ESTABLISHED. 

Concrete printed example (PAGE). Noorduin's appendix table, p. 303 of the thesis, reads off Beethoven's String Quartet op. 132, second movement: 3/4 "Allegro ma non tanto," dotted half note = 58 (the table notes this was misprinted in the sources); then alla breve "L'istesso timpo" [sic, as in the table], whole note = 58. A dotted half is one 3/4 bar; a whole note is one 2/2 bar. So the bar is held, and the quarter changes from a third of the bar to a quarter of it. This is a bar-equals-bar relationship, marked by word and by number. Source: Noorduin thesis (listed below), page 303 rendered and read. Bar numbers: NOT ESTABLISHED.

The same table, op. 133 (PAGE): 6/8 Allegro dotted quarter = 126, 2/4 Meno mosso quarter = 72, alla breve Allegro quarter = 132, 6/8 Allegro molto dotted quarter = 132. No single constant holds across that sequence. That is the table's content, not a claim about intent.

## Answer 3: the default when nothing is printed

Per era, with evidence. I found no source that states a default in so many words for any era. What follows separates what sources show from inference.

**Pre-1700 and the proportional tradition.** Signs encoded relationships. Caplin (QUOTE, Cambridge Histories, 2008, ch. 21, p. 659): "three minims sound within the same time span as two prior minims" (sesquialtera, 3:2), and "various triple-meter signatures evolved out of proportional signs, such as 3/2 and 3/4." DeFord (ABSTRACT, Early Music History 14, 1995): "The signs of these proportions ostensibly specify precise tempo relationships between the binary and ternary passages," read in several ways by period, region, and context. So the mathematical-relationship model is real, early, and disputed. Caplin: my fetch returned a model-generated summary of a McGill-hosted PDF, so treat the wording as summary-level (https://www.music.mcgill.ca/~caplin/18th19th-c-rhythm-and-meter.pdf). DeFord: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-music-history/article/abs/tempo-relationships-between-duple-and-triple-time-in-the-sixteenth-century/088EA7AB4A6FE1CBAD7937264B9E0D29

**Eighteenth century.** Tempo was tied to the metre itself, not to a relationship between metres. Kirnberger, quoted by Noorduin (QUOTE, secondary, thesis section on Beethoven's library): metres "having larger values, like alla breve, 3/2, and 6/4 metre, have a heavier and slower tempo than those of smaller values, like 2/4, 3/4, and 6/8, and these in turn are less lively that 3/8 or 6/16 metre." Caplin (QUOTE, p. 669) records Kirnberger on alla breve, "performed twice as fast as its note values indicate." Wikipedia's Time signature entry (ABSTRACT) says tempo giusto "dates to the Baroque era." INFERENCE: under tempo giusto a change of signature itself implies a tempo change, so a constant eighth is not this period's default.

**Classical and Romantic.** Caplin (QUOTE, p. 669) says later theorists "largely separate these domains, especially the connection between meter and tempo." So by the nineteenth century the sign alone no longer fixed a tempo. Noorduin (QUOTE, thesis introduction, citing Rosen) reports that a 2:1 proportion between sections fits Beethoven's op. 111 but that "there are plenty of examples in which Beethoven's metronome marks do not follow this proportion," giving the Fourth Symphony, Adagio introduction against Allegro vivace, as one (the note values in my text extraction were lost; the numbers 66 and 80 are as extracted). Rosen's text: not read. No source states a Classical or Romantic default for an unmarked change.

**Late nineteenth-century Russian.** NOT ESTABLISHED. I found no Russian source or Russian-repertoire discussion of this question.

**Twentieth century and contemporary.** Metric modulation is a relationship derived from "a value heard before the change." Its example keeps a minimal denominator constant: "the minimal denominator, such as the eighth note in a 4/4 to 3/8 change, 'remains constant in duration'" (QUOTE, Wikipedia, ABSTRACT). That supports Dann's hypothesis for one case only. Wikipedia's Time signature entry (ABSTRACT) says irrational signatures can be avoided "by specifying a relationship" between note lengths in successive bars. 

**Verdict on the hypothesis.**
- "Contemporary scores keep a constant microbeat": not established as a default; the sources show the relationship is printed. REFINED.
- "Older scores relate mathematically": supported for the proportional tradition (Caplin, DeFord) and for Beethoven's op. 132 bar-equals-bar relationship. But the relationship differs by case (bar held there; beat held by l'istesso's definition; no single constant in op. 133). PARTLY CONFIRMED, PARTLY CONTRADICTED: the maths exist; the constant is not the eighth.
- "Sometimes signalled, sometimes inferred": confirmed by the existence of l'istesso tempo and equations and by the absence of any stated default. INFERENCE: that l'istesso tempo exists at all suggests unmarked changes were not read as beat-preserving; I did not find a source that says so.

## Answer 4: 3/4 to 9/8, 2/4 to 6/8, C to 12/8

What the sources give, with no printed signal:

- Two live conventions, both named. Baylor open textbook (ABSTRACT, https://openbooks.library.baylor.edu/rhythm/?p=178): "there are two ways that can happen—one with the beat constant and one with the division constant." Beat constant, 2/4 to 6/8: the quarter equals the dotted quarter, and "the 6/8 eighth notes in the figure above actually move faster than the 2/4 eighth notes." Division constant: the eighth is constant and "the beat has to get longer to accommodate the extra eighth note." A second Baylor chapter (https://openbooks.library.baylor.edu/rhythm/chapter/23/) uses eighth = eighth, with 2/4 at quarter = 180 slowing in 6/8. Teaching texts; they present both and give no rule for choosing.
- Beat held. l'istesso tempo's definition (onmusic, QUOTE) is beat-constant. For 3/4 to 9/8 under that reading, the old quarter becomes the new dotted quarter: the bar keeps its length and the eighths become triplets in the space of the old beats. 
- Bar held. Op. 132, 3/4 to 2/2 (PAGE) holds the bar, not the beat or the eighth.
- Eighth held (Baylor's division-constant case). 3/4 to 9/8 gives nine eighths against six, so the new bar is one and a half times the old.

When each applies. The sources I reached give no decision rule. INFERENCE only, from the evidence collected: a printed l'istesso tempo or equation decides; otherwise the cue is the notated note values. Lau (ABSTRACT, EUROMAC 9 postprint, https://creaa.unistra.fr/websites/gream/Activites/Euromac_2017_-_Postprint_-_Extended_abstract_-_LAU_Wing.pdf) says that in German lieder a change of perceived tempo at a new metre of "three or more measures" is linked to "changes in note values." It concerns perceived tempo, not a notated rule.

C to 12/8: no source found that treats this pair. 4/4 to 3/2: NOT ESTABLISHED for the Romantic period; Caplin's sesquialtera note (QUOTE) is the mensural 3:2 proportion, not a nineteenth-century finding, and I do not carry it across.

No concrete published example with bar numbers was found for 3/4 to 9/8, 2/4 to 6/8 (apart from the constructed Baylor examples, which are not repertoire), or C to 12/8. See the last section.

## Answer 5: what Ilya would need

(a) Persistence rule. A printed time signature holds until the next one is printed, including across systems; a courtesy signature at a system end announces a change that takes effect after the barline. Source: Gould p. 152. Mixed-metre signatures with "+" or a hyphen at the outset are a separate form (Gould p. 179).

(b) Printed signals worth reading from a scan.
1. The word "L'istesso tempo" (beat constant, per the onmusic definition).
2. A note-equals-note equation at the change (for example ♩ = ♪, or ♩. = ♩), which is the standard form named in the Wikipedia summary.
3. A new metronome mark at the changed metre (Beethoven's op. 132 is the evidence that a number can override any default).
4. "Doppio movimento" or "doppio più lento" (exactly double or half, per the weak glossary source).

(c) A defensible default when none is printed. The project's own ruling 6 (eighth constant, e20-handover section 4) is a reasonable default for the corpus, since a constant smallest unit is the one choice that does not require inventing a ratio. I state it as a default, not as a finding: the sources show it is one of two conventions and that Beethoven's marks sometimes hold the bar instead. Condition for departing from it: a printed signal from (b), or a singer's override. INFERENCE: a note-value density check (do the new eighths or triplets carry the surface rhythm the old beats carried?) could flag a probable beat-held reading, but no source states it as a test, so it belongs in the uncertainty display, not the computation.

(d) Where the singer must be asked or allowed to choose. Every unmarked simple-to-compound change (2/4 to 6/8, 3/4 to 9/8, C to 12/8): the two conventions differ by a factor of 3/2 in bar length (3/4 to 9/8) or 2/3 in eighth length. Unmarked 4/4 to 3/2. Any change where the felt beat changes unit. Baylor presents both conventions as available, and Beethoven's marks fit neither alone.
