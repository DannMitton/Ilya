## RQ2. What are the two elements that meet on every sung note?

**§ 2. What is a pitch?**
Every note you sing is a vibration. How many times your vocal folds meet each second is its frequency, measured in hertz; the time each vibration takes is its period. Frequency and period belong to the air. Pitch, the note you hear, exists only in the brain. For a sung note the two match so closely that musicians name one by the other, and MELODY does too, rounding each frequency to the nearest key on the staff. A sung note is never a single frequency, either. It carries a series of higher frequencies, called harmonics, each a whole-number multiple of the note. Heard as notes, the first lies an octave above, the next an octave and a fifth above, then two octaves above. Your ear fuses the lowest five to eight of them into the one note you hear, and hears the rest as a bright buzz.
[Howell 2025, 47 to 51; Mitton 2020, § 5.3.3]

**§ 3. What is a vowel, to the ear?**
Think of a vowel as a shape of your vocal tract, the tube from your vocal folds to your lips. Each shape favours certain frequencies, letting them ring longer and louder; these favoured frequencies are its resonances. The lowest is the first resonance, written fR1, and it largely sets how open a vowel sounds: the more open the vowel, the higher its fR1. Hold the shape, and the resonances stay where they are, whatever note you sing. You do not hear a resonance as a note. You hear it as colour, the quality that makes [ɑ] sound like [ɑ]. In my own voice, [ɑ] resonates at 617 Hz, near D♯5, and [i] at 296 Hz, near D4. Your values are your own, measured from vocal fry by a method Donald Miller developed.
[Bozeman 2025, 21, 27 to 33; Bozeman 2010, 293; Howell 2025, 131 to 133; Mitton 2020, §§ 5.3.1, 5.3.3]

## RQ3. What happens when they meet?

**§ 4. How do pitch and vowel meet?**
Sing up a scale on one vowel. The vowel's resonances stay put while the note and all its harmonics climb past them, and each time a harmonic crosses a resonance, the colour shifts. Since the harmonics sit at fixed intervals above the note, these crossings fall at fixed intervals below the resonance: two octaves and a major third, two octaves, an octave and a fifth, and the most important, an octave. The lowest harmonics, the ones that fuse into the pitch you hear, are the same ones that meet the vowel's resonance.
[Bozeman 2025, 26, 44 to 45; Howell 2025, 48]

**§ 5. What is the pitch of turning, and what do open and close timbre sound like?**
The strongest crossing comes when the harmonic an octave above your note reaches the vowel's first resonance. That note, an octave below fR1, is the vowel's pitch of turning. Below it, your tone is in open timbre: clear, bright, direct. Above it, your tone turns to close timbre: rounder, smoother, domed, somewhat covered. Timbre here means the overall colour of a sound. Older traditions call these qualities voce aperta and voce chiusa, and the turn itself covering. Close timbre is not a closed vowel: a closed vowel is a matter of diction, the tongue's height in [e] against [ɛ], while close timbre depends on the note you sing.

The turn is a zone of about a major second to a major third, not a single note, and it differs by vowel: in my voice, [ɑ] turns near D♯4 and [i] near D3, more than an octave apart. Hold the vowel's shape through the turn, and the vowel you hear grows subtly rounder. That is passive vowel migration.

The turn itself is acoustic, and it can happen with no change in the larynx at all. But the vocal tract pushes back on the vibrating folds, and it pushes differently on either side of a resonance; that is why the break from "chest" to "head" is often provoked at the turn. Skilful classical singers minimize the break, other styles feature it, and changing the vowel or your loudness can move it.

Listen for it: the flip in Sarah McLachlan, Alanis Morissette, Dolores O'Riordan, Sinéad O'Connor, Ella Fitzgerald, Sarah Vaughan, Lauryn Hill, and country yodelling (Dann's examples, 2026-11-04 00:41; spellings to verify). Then listen to a classical singer cross the same zone. [Classical example: Dann's to choose.]
[Bozeman 2025, 45 to 50, 67; Bozeman 2010, 293 to 295; Titze 2012, 543 to 544; Howell 2025, 151, 202; Mitton 2020, §§ 1.7, 3.2.4, 3.2.5, 5.3.3]

**§ 6. What else happens as a melody climbs?**
Below the pitch of turning, higher harmonics cross fR1 too, at an octave and a fifth, two octaves, and two octaves and a major third below it. Each brings a smaller turning, subtler because the next harmonic down trails close behind. At the other end, when the note you sing reaches fR1 itself, the tone becomes whoop: a full, hooting colour dominated by the note itself. Treble voices sing much of their range there. Lower voices reach it rarely, mainly on [i], [u], and [y] near the top of their range. Yell is a different path: pulling fR1 upward to stay with the harmonic an octave above the note. Musical theatre, popular, and folk singing use it often, and Western classical singing avoids it.

Between the turn and whoop, you can shape the vowel three ways. Close it further, and you reach whoop sooner, with a headier, fuller sound; trained treble voices often choose this. Keep its shape, and let it migrate. Or open it gradually, which postpones whoop and keeps a buzzier, more robust colour; lower voices often choose this. Many professional singers also seek extra ring in this span from the vowel's second resonance.
[Bozeman 2025, 45 to 49, 52 to 56, 60, 71 to 72; Mitton 2020, §§ 3.2.5, 5.3.4]

**§ 7. Is there another way to picture this?**
Everything so far describes sound as a set of frequencies. You can also picture it in time. Each time your vocal folds meet, they set the air in your vocal tract ringing, the way a push sets a swing moving, and the ringing fades until the next meeting. The vowel's resonance is how fast that air rings. At the pitch of turning, it completes exactly two swings in each vibration of the folds. Sing lower, and it completes more than two. Sing higher, and the next meeting of the folds cuts the second swing short: that interruption is the turn. When the note reaches fR1, there is time for only one swing. The same picture shows why low and high voices differ: a low note's long vibration leaves room for many swings, a high note's short one for few.

Howell argues the time picture is closer to the physical event, while the frequency picture summarizes its result. Bozeman surveys several such models side by side. Each is useful, and each leaves something out. MELODY's marks come from the frequency picture.
[Howell 2025, 67 to 77, 151; Bozeman 2025, 21 to 26]

## RQ4. How does MELODY show these meetings, and how far can its forecast be trusted?

**§ 8. What does MELODY show me, and how do I use it?**
MELODY pairs each sung note with a small stemless lavender notehead, drawn at that note's pitch of turning. It is worked out from your fR1 for the vowel the score sets on that note, and rounded to the nearest key. A solid head comes from your own fry; a hollow head is a placeholder until you record one. The stem shows which side of the turn the note falls on: stem down for open timbre, stem up for close. The stem is decided on exact frequencies, so where a note shares a line or space with its lavender head, trust the stem. A red box marks a note where the voice literature singles out a coordination demand, such as a note meeting fR1 itself; INSIGHTS describes each one.

Before you sing, you can see where each vowel will turn, and choose: let it turn rather than push through, plan your registration, and decide how to shape the vowel above it. Then sing, and compare what you hear and feel with what MELODY forecast.
[Mitton 2020, Appendices B and C; Bozeman 2025, 51, 68; code read 2026-11-03: `packages/score-parser/src/overlay-engine.ts:76-81`, `:163-170`; `staff-renderer.ts:2139-2143`; `GuideContent.svelte:378`]

**§ 9. Where does this picture stop?**
MELODY's marks are a forecast, not a recording. The turn is a zone, while the staff can show only a single key. These effects are strongest in loud, high singing and fade when you sing quietly. Your values are your own, and they move with how you shape each vowel: open a vowel, and its pitch of turning rises with it, though MELODY draws the vowel as written. Every model leaves something out. Use the marks to know where to listen, and let your ear decide.
[Howell 2025, 15, 151; Bozeman 2010, 295; Bozeman 2025, 51; Mitton 2020, § 5.3.4]
