**"Surprising" sentences, quoted verbatim (p. 66):**

- "On the other hand, it is surprising to find that the performance length, recordings, and the difficulty level of a piece are seldom given, since they are useful annotations for both singer and teacher." (p. 66)

So: **performance length is confirmed among the least-common annotations**, grouped explicitly with score length, recordings, and difficulty level (p. 66), and she calls it "surprising" that performance length (along with recordings and difficulty level) is seldom given.

**Four categories and which annotation types sit in which (pp. 67–68):**

- **Informative** (p. 67): "title of the piece, the composer's name, the composer's birth and death dates, information about the composer, the poet's name, the poet's birth and death dates, information about the poet, dedications, language, the year of composition, the premiere, the context in which the piece was created, the sources of the musical score, authoritative performances of the piece, and available recordings."
- **Descriptive** (p. 67–68): "the range of the vocal line, the tessitura, voice type, brief poetic synopsis, text of the poem, translation of the poem, the International Phonetic Alphabet equivalency (IPA), musical form, key signature, tempo and meter indications, melody, harmony, rhythm, dynamics, piano accompaniment, and the style of text setting." (Note: "brief poetic synopsis" appears in this sentence though it is not one of the 39 listed annotation types in Table 1 — **NOT ESTABLISHED** whether this is a discrepancy in her text or an omission from Table 1; flagged, p. 67–68.)
- **Evaluative** (p. 68): "the level of difficulty, the character or mood of the piece, technical demands, pedagogical comments, errors in the score, and the performance length of the piece."
- **Prescriptive** (p. 68): "the author's recommendations regarding the interpretation of a piece, and may include suggestions for all aspects of performance, including tempo, dynamics, breath markings, interpretation, prosody adjustments, revisions to the score, as well as lyric diction elements to consider such as liaisons or elisions."

**Three modes of presentation in voice guides (p. 68):** systematic (repeated point-form list per song), narrative (continuous prose, varying annotations), and a combination of the two.

### 3.1.2 Review of Existing Difficulty Level Assessment Methods (pp. 68–71)

No acknowledged standardized method exists for grading vocal-composition difficulty (p. 68). Methods and authors reviewed:

1. **Most voice guides/syllabi give no explanation of method** (p. 69): named examples — the Royal Conservatory of Music's Voice Syllabus; Judith Carman's "Music Reviews" in the Journal of Singing; Alan Ord's Songs for Beginning Bass Voice; the Alliance for Canadian New Music Projects Voice Syllabus; the Associated Board of the Royal Schools of Music Voice Syllabus. Criticism: they provide no explanation of method.

2. **Joan Frey Boytim, Solo Vocal Repertoire for Young Singers** (p. 69). Method: three degrees — I-Easy, II-Moderate, III-Advanced, "determined by a scale based on the singer's ability without much previous vocal training. For instance, III is for the advanced teenager. (This could, however, translate as moderate for the college sophomore.)" (quoted, p. 69). Criticism (p. 69): "problematic on two levels. First, trying to determine the degree of difficulty of a vocal piece based on the singer's ability without much previous vocal training is unmeasurable and an almost impossible criterion to apply, as each singer possesses different abilities and a unique training background. Second, trying to correlate age and level of difficulty is problematic since all voices are unique, and shaped by different backgrounds." Also faulted for not accounting for dynamics, text length, and difficulty of the piano accompaniment (p. 69).

3. **J. Arden Hopkin, Songs for Young Singers: An Annotated List for Developing Voices** (p. 70). Called "a more complete and measurable method." Seven values: melodic contour, phrasing, language, rhythm, accompaniment, harmonic language, and dynamics (note: text lists only these — see below). Example given for melodic contour: small range (octave or less) = 1 point; moderate range (octave and a half or less) = 2 points; wide range (more than one and a half octave) = 3 points; high tessitura adds 1 point. Sum of scores across all seven values determines level: elementary (7–11), intermediate (12–16), advanced (above 17) (p. 70). Criticism (p. 70): "it does not take into account all elements that can influence the difficulty level of a piece, such as large interval leaps, ornamentation, articulation, unprepared modulations, long texts, mature or comical subjects, the use of an old form of language, and the absence of a prelude."
   - NOT ESTABLISHED: the sentence "Hopkin takes into consideration seven values: melodic contour, phrasing, language, rhythm, accompaniment, harmonic language, and dynamics" lists only six distinct terms after "seven values" as extracted (melodic contour, phrasing, language, rhythm, accompaniment, harmonic language, dynamics — that is in fact seven; confirmed by direct count, no missing term). No error here.

4. **Christopher Arneson, Literature for Teaching: A Guide for Choosing Solo Vocal Repertoire from a Developmental Perspective** (pp. 70–71). Four sample rubrics; each element scored 1–4 (Rubric 1) or 1–5 (Rubrics 2, 3, 4), lowest = beginning skills, highest = advanced skills. ### 4.1 The accent d'insistance (pp. 89–90)

Definition, quoted (p. 89): "The accent d'insistance (accent of insistence) is a non-mandatory stress in French that allows the speaker to emphasize a particular word, to orient the meaning of a statement, or to add expressivity. It is generally made by increasing the intensity and duration of either the first syllable, the first consonant, or the first vowel of a word." Source: Pierre Léon, *Phonétisme et prononciations du français*, 6th ed. (Paris: Armand Colin, 2011), 158–159 — cited for the claim that there are five ways to perform the accent d'insistance.

**The five diction rules, with her worked examples (quoted), pp. 89–90:**
1. First syllable louder — example: "L'absence," line "Tu m'apparais, pressant encor ma main," first syllable *pres* [prɛ] of *pressant* emphasized.
2. First consonant is a sibilant → consonant is stretched out — example: "Sol canadien," line "Salut, ô sublimes montagnes," initial *s* of *sublimes* stretched (*sssublimes*).
3. First consonant is a plosive → consonant preceded by a glottal stop — example: "Laissez-moi dormir," line "Ni désespoir, ni larme amère !", the *d* of *désespoir* preceded by a glottal stop.
4. Word begins with a vowel → vowel preceded by a glottal stop — example: "Épanchement," word *errante* in "Sais-tu que ma pauvre âme, errante et solitaire," glottal stop before initial *e*.
5. Word begins with a vowel and is part of a liaison/elision with the preceding word → the linking consonant is emphasized, following the consonant/plosive rules — example: "Le jour de l'an à la campagne," line "Cette autre jeunesse du cœur," the *t* of *cette* pronounced with the *au* of *autre*, preceded by a glottal stop.

Footnote convention (p. 90): a footnote marks uncommon cases where the *second* syllable (not the first) is emphasized.

### 4.2 French Canadian Pronunciation (pp. 90–92)

Two registers: colloquial and formal (p. 90). Colloquial: "contains many vowel diphthongs and word contractions," used informally, "perceived as a language of lesser quality," linked to socio-economic factors (p. 90). Source quoted: Luc Ostiguy and Claude Tousignant, *Les prononciations du français québécois* (Montreal: Guérin universitaire 3e millénaire, 2008), 19 — "In Montreal, the diphthongized pronunciation of the vowel a . . . characterizes a speaker who is less educated or a speaker who does not work in a position requiring daily communication with the public."

Formal register: equivalent to formal France French, taught historically by clergy using French-published texts and dictionaries (p. 90). Since most 19th-c. French Canadian songs were composed for an educated audience, **the songs in this chapter are pronounced in the formal register**, except where colloquial/folkloric — the one example named is "Vir' de bord, mon ami Pierre," arranged by Emmanuel Blain de Saint-Aubin, "which features word contractions, indicating its association with the colloquial form of the French Canadian language" (p. 90).

Additional notes (pp. 91–92): affrication of consonants *d* and *t* before *i*/*u* (a feature of today's colloquial pronunciation) is **absent** from early-20th-c. recordings of French Canadian folkloric songs and so was **not included** in her pronunciation scheme — an explicit set-aside, quoted: "Since the affrication of consonants d and t is absent from vocal performances of French Canadian folkloric songs in the early twentieth century recordings, this characteristic has not been included." (p. 91–92). Similarly, the uvular r [ʁ] (also a modern colloquial feature) replaced the flipped r [r] only in the 1930s onward (per Ostiguy and Tousignant, 167, and corroborated by 1920s recordings of Charles Marchand, Alexandre Desmarteaux, and Conrad Gauthier); **since all songs in the chapter were composed in the 19th century, only the flipped r [r] is used** (p. 92) — a second explicit methodological set-aside.

### Chapter 4 closing summary (p. 176)

Quoted in full: "In summary, even though many French Canadian vocal compositions were intended to be performed by amateur musicians, the assessment of their difficulty levels showed that the majority of these compositions lie in intermediate and advanced difficulty levels, which require a high degree of musicianship skills from both singer and pianist. Such results indicate that composers either wrote challenging pieces for the amateur performer or that the amateur performers of the time possessed a certain level of musical training. The extended performance lengths of several French Canadian songs show that the repeated stanzas and refrain form was a dominant form in the nineteenth century. In contrast, in the latter half of the nineteenth century, as composers began to abandon this form, performance lengths shortened. Interestingly, only one song used the colloquial form of French Canadian language, 'Vir' de bord, mon ami Pierre' by Blain de Saint-Aubin, which may be explained by the fact that this song is strongly influenced by French Canadian folklore. The majority of French Canadian songs for solo voice and piano, however, feature poetry written in the formal form of the French language."

## PART 3 — The Conclusion (pp. 177–179)

### Recommendations for future work, quoted

1. **Recordings/performance-tradition research** (p. 178): "It would be relevant to pursue further research on recordings of French Canadian vocal repertoire, in order to create an exhaustive discography extant, and to determine additional characteristics of its performance tradition; for example, when the affrication of the d and t and the uvular r appeared in songs of folkloric influence, or if there are differences in the performance traditions of French and French Canadian mélodies."

2. **Extend the difficulty grid to other genres** (p. 178): "the grid could easily serve as the basis for the development of level of difficulty assessments applicable to other vocal genres such as opera, chamber music, or early music."

3. **Broader adoption of a quantifiable difficulty scale** (p. 178): "The creation of a quantifiable level of difficulty assessment scale across the fields of voice performance and vocal pedagogy is essential for establishing consistent and efficient difficulty level ratings of vocal pieces... such a scale may help to address the issue highlighted by pedagogue John Nix—the assigning of inappropriate repertoire can hamper a student's rate of development."

4. **Broader curricular adoption** (p. 179): "one hopes that French Canadian songs for voice and piano will no longer be ignored or forgotten, but might be studied alongside French mélodie in the singer's curriculum, and appreciated for their historical, artistic and pedagogical value."

### Limitations stated, quoted

1. **Limited access to performance tradition** (p. 178): "Even though this paper unveils information about where a small number of French Canadian songs were performed, there is limited access to the performance tradition of this repertoire, especially with regard to how these songs were taught or how they were performed. A small number of recordings of this repertoire exist that can help elucidate the performance tradition..."

2. **Scope limitation of the difficulty grid** (p. 178): "the creation of a level of difficulty assessment grid was limited to compositions for solo voice and piano" (implicitly not validated for other vocal genres — addressed by recommendation 2 above).

3. (Restated from Chapter 3, referenced again here, p. 177): "no standard methodology exists to rate the level of difficulty of solo vocal pieces" prior to her grid — framed as the gap her dissertation addresses, not a limitation of her own work per se.

### Summary of chapter contents (p. 177–178)

Inventory of "close to 200 pieces" of 19th-century French Canadian solo vocal repertoire (p. 177). Two main research objectives: (1) introduce French Canadian solo vocal compositions; (2) guide performers/teachers in choice and interpretation of the repertoire (p. 177). Chapter 4's annotations "illustrated the pedagogical and performance value of this repertoire" and addressed "concerns of faulty prosody... by avoiding emphasis on the mute e, and by performing adequate elisions or liaisons that may not have been written in the vocal part" (p. 178). Notes the recent digitization of Canadian archives (BAnQ, Early Canadiana Online, Hathi Trust, Internet Archive) as having newly enabled this kind of research (p. 179).
